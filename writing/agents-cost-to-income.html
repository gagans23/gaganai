How to use agents to cut your bank’s cost-to-income ratio

Agents move a bank’s cost-to-income ratio mostly through costs it avoids as it grows, rather than costs it cuts. Start with six kinds of work, find the judgement floor, and price the work rather than the teams. Then look at where your bank starts: at 55% the cost case carries itself; at 28% agents have to earn their keep through growth, margins and deposits.

A letter of credit for a cargo leaving Jebel Ali for Mundra arrives with an invoice, a bill of lading, a certificate of origin, an insurance certificate and a packing list. Under UCP 600 the bank has at most five banking days to decide whether the documents comply. Most of the work in that window is reading and cross-checking names, dates, weights and ports. The judgement that matters, whether a small mismatch is a real discrepancy and what to do about it, usually takes an experienced officer a few minutes.

Most bank AI programmes are a list of use cases, and some run to hundreds. Each one goes after a piece of a process like this one, and each can succeed while the cost-to-income ratio barely moves, because the steps on either side of it haven’t changed. The banks that get real value from agents will work from a much shorter list: six kinds of work that every business shares, described before anyone asks what agents should do.

The idea in four steps
  1. Describe the bank as six kinds of work. Five of them are execution, and agents are making execution cheap. The sixth, assist, supports judgement that stays with a person. Build one capability for each kind and reuse it across every business.
  2. Once agents do the execution, most of what a case still costs is the time a person spends checking it. That is the judgement floor. Cheaper models barely move it; only trust, earned with evidence, does.
  3. Price shared work by the unit, so savings are counted once and land in the business that buys the work. As the bank grows, its costs stay roughly flat, and that is where most of the fall in the ratio comes from.
  4. Know where you start. At about 55%, where US and European banks run, the cost a bank avoids with agents carries the case on its own. At about 28%, where the UAE’s largest banks run, that prize is half the size, and agents earn their keep by absorbing growth, holding the ratio as margins fall and keeping deposits.

01 · SignalSix kinds of work

Nearly everything a bank does falls into six kinds of work. I define them by what starts the work and what it produces, rather than by department, so that a trade officer in one bank and a mortgage processor in another can be described in the same terms.

Kind of workStarts withProduces
CaptureA document, form or conversationVerified data
AssistA person who has to judge, advise or sellA better decision, still made by that person
ProcessAn instructionA completed transaction
InvestigateSomething that doesn’t fit: an alert, a break, a disputeA resolution and the reason for it
RespondA question from a client or colleagueAn answer
AssureAn obligation to prove somethingEvidence

The letter of credit contains all six of them.

Most jobs combine several kinds, in proportions that vary a great deal. Relationship managers are hired to advise, which is assist work, but much of their week goes on notes, queries and moving deals through approval. A trade operations officer’s week is weighted the other way.

Agents take over tasks, and jobs are rebuilt from what remains. In the chart no role disappears, but every one gets smaller. The relationship manager and the credit analyst end up spending more than half of what remains on assist work. The trade operations role shrinks the most, to about a third of its time today, so the work left in three such jobs can often be done by one person.

This is also how agents scale across a bank. A trade officer, a mortgage processor and a compliance analyst do the same six things in different proportions, so the bank doesn’t need a separate agent for every use case on its list. It needs one capability for each kind of work, built well and given different context in each business. A document reader built for letters of credit can read mortgage files next, with that business’s rules and a different person owning the decision. Each new business then adds context to something that already works, and the cost of the next use case falls instead of starting again from zero.

02What gets cheap, and what doesn’t

Five of the six are execution: capture, process, investigate, respond and assure. Assist is different because the work it supports stays with a person, and the rest of my argument rests on that distinction. The cost of execution is falling quickly. The cost of judgement is not, and neither is the cost of the two things judgement relies on in a bank: its knowledge of its own rules and clients, and the trust that allows it to act for them.

Epoch AI estimates that the price of reaching a given level of AI capability has been falling by between 9 and 900 times a year, depending on the task. For GPT-4’s level of performance on PhD-level science questions, it fell about 40 times a year (Epoch AI). That doesn’t make AI budgets smaller, because cheaper capability gets used for many more steps in each task. For planning, the useful figure is the full cost of a task.

Take a periodic KYC refresh for a small-business client. Done by hand, it takes an analyst about 90 minutes, or roughly $90. If agents read the documents, run the screening and draft the assessment, and the analyst spends ten minutes reviewing, the cost comes to about $12, of which compute is $1.72. The figures are illustrative.

More than 80% of the remaining cost is the analyst’s ten minutes, which is what I mean by the judgement floor. It is judgement too, but the review a person does inside execution work, not the advising and deciding of assist work. Halving the price of every token would save under a dollar. Moving from reviewing every case to reviewing a sample would save far more. As volumes grow, the cost above the floor keeps falling towards it, and the only way below it is to trust the agent with more of the decision.

Software teams reached this point first. Faros AI’s study of more than 10,000 developers found that teams using AI heavily merged 98% more pull requests, while review time rose 91% and delivery metrics hardly changed (Philipp Dubach, citing Faros AI).

03What the work leaves behind

When people do the work, most of what they learn stays with them. Which discrepancies a particular applicant tends to waive, which counterparty always sends the insurance certificate late, which rule causes most of the rework: a little of it ends up in emails and case notes, and the rest leaves when the person moves on. It is the problem I called the amnesia tax: the answer is kept and the judgement behind it is lost.

When agents carry a case from intake to decision, a complete record comes as a by-product. Every document read, every rule applied, every exception raised and every human decision, with the reason given for it, is kept and linked to the case. Over a few years that becomes an institutional memory banks have never really had. A new credit officer can see how similar cases were decided and why, a policy change can be tested against last year’s cases before it goes live, and a regulator’s question about a decision made eighteen months ago can be answered from the record rather than from someone’s recollection.

The same record gives the bank something close to a working twin of its own operations. Agents now carry work across functions and keep the context that used to die at the seams between them, an argument I made in Enterprises are organised into functions. Work is not. A letter of credit no longer disappears into the gaps between trade operations, sanctions screening and the relationship team. The bank can see where cases wait, which hand-offs fail, and what a change in one team would do to the others, and it can try a change on the model before trying it on customers. In time it may also hold something like a twin of its most experienced people’s judgement. That would not replace them, but it could show a junior officer how a senior one tends to weigh a case.

Memory of this kind has to be designed. It needs an owner, rules on what is kept and for how long, protection for customer data, and regular checks that past decisions are not quietly hardening into rules nobody chose. A bank that does this well builds an asset that grows with every case and that no competitor can buy. It is also the most practical answer to the training problem discussed further on, because some of the experience juniors used to gain by doing the work can come from studying how it was done.

04What changes for customers

For customers, the first change is speed. Accounts and working-capital lines can be opened in hours rather than days, queries can be answered at night, and fewer customers are asked to resend documents or have an account frozen because an alert was handled badly.

The larger effect is on who receives good service. Advice has always been limited by the cost of a banker’s time, so it has concentrated among the largest clients. If preparation, follow-up and paperwork cost very little, a bank can offer a small-business owner or a mid-income household something closer to what private clients get, and design products around their circumstances rather than a segment average.

Customers will also use agents of their own, and those agents will not leave money idle out of habit. McKinsey estimates that about $23 trillion of the world’s $70 trillion in consumer deposits is held in accounts paying close to nothing (American Banker, citing McKinsey). Software that moves those balances to better rates benefits customers directly.

Some of the risk falls on customers too. An agent’s mistake can be repeated across thousands of cases before it is caught, and a customer who is wrongly declined is owed an explanation from a person. For that reason I would apply two rules without exception: every agent has a named person accountable for it, and no agent checks its own work.

05What changes for the bank

Most of the six show up first as lower cost, faster service or lower risk. Assist shows up as revenue, because bankers who spend less time on preparation and paperwork can cover more clients and prepare better for each one, and a programme that tracks only cost will miss it.

Over five years the bank’s shape changes. In an illustrative model based on these shares, agents end up doing 58% of the work. With the business growing, total work rises by about half and headcount settles around 15% lower, mostly because people who leave are not replaced. Headcount falls by less than the work handed over, because the people who stay take on the review, supervision and exception handling that come with agents, and bankers spend more of their time with clients. Salaries fall while spending on computing rises, so the cost base changes in composition more than in size.

Where a bank starts matters. US banks ran at a cost-to-income ratio of 55.6% in 2025 (FDIC) and EU banks at 53.3% (EBA). The UAE’s ten largest banks ran at 28.1% (Alvarez & Marsal), roughly half the Western level. In the same model, a bank at the US and European average of about 55% ends near 41% after five years, and a bank at the UAE average ends near 21%. In both cases the cost base falls only about 3%.

The income growth behind those numbers, about 30% over five years or a little over 5% a year, is the bank’s own, and agents shouldn’t get the credit for it. What agents do is let the bank handle half as much work again without its costs rising to match. A plan without agents would usually assume costs grow roughly in line with income, and on that assumption both ratios stay where they started. The gain shows up as cost avoided rather than cost removed, so a programme judged only on cuts to today’s cost base will miss most of it. For a UAE bank the fall is about seven points rather than fourteen, though in proportion it is the same: about a quarter below where the ratio would otherwise be.

Competition limits how much of this a bank keeps. Every bank can buy the same models, and McKinsey expects most of the efficiency gains to be competed away, as they were with digital banking. It estimates that if banks don’t change their business models, global banking profits of about $1.2 trillion could shrink by as much as 10% (McKinsey). Deposits that stay put out of inertia are especially exposed: in the same research, a move of 5 to 10% of checking accounts to top-of-market rates would be worth about 20% of the industry’s global profits (American Banker).

What a bank can hold on to is what competitors cannot buy: the judgement of its people, its knowledge of its clients and its own rules, the memory of how it has decided, and the trust it has built with clients and regulators. I have written before about why the value of deployed AI depends on the knowledge beneath it (The Empty Window, The Search Tax) and about the layers no vendor can supply (The Layers You Cannot Buy).

06 · Lean banksWhen there is nothing left to cut

Figure 6 gives the high-cost bank twice the gain: fourteen points of ratio against seven, and roughly twice the profit on the same income. On the usual cost case, agents look like a bigger prize for banks in the US and Europe than for banks in the UAE. A lean bank that judges agents only on the cost they take out will find the smaller prize and may decide they aren’t worth much.

That is the wrong test for a bank at 28%. Its case rests on three problems the ratio hides. Its work is growing faster than it can absorb without hiring. The interest-rate tailwind that helped earn the low ratio is fading. And part of its profit depends on deposits that customers’ own agents will try to move.

Same six kinds of work, different value

The six kinds of work don’t change when the ratio does. A trade officer in Dubai does the same capture and investigate work as one in Frankfurt. What changes is what each kind of work is worth to the bank.

At 55%, almost every kind of work is first a cost line, and the cost an agent avoids is reason enough to deploy it. At 28%, each kind answers one of the three problems. Capture, process, investigate and assure grow with the balance sheet, so agents there buy capacity. Assist is where income comes from when margins narrow. Respond is part of how a bank keeps its deposits: rate is not the only reason money stays, and service is the reason the bank controls every day.

For each kind of work, the agent does the same job at both banks. What differs is what the bank gets from it, and so what it should measure.

Growth is the cost problem

The UAE’s ten largest banks grew loans by 20.7% in 2025 and deposits by 16.6% (Alvarez & Marsal). Operating income rose 10.6%. Costs aren’t published as a growth figure, but with the cost-to-income ratio moving from 28.9% to 28.1% while income rose 10.6%, the implied rise is about 7.5%.

Every new loan and account brings work with it, most of it capture, process, investigate and assure: onboarding and KYC, payments, screening alerts, and the evidence a regulator expects. Most of that work is still done by people, so costs follow the balance sheet up, even if more slowly. The ratio fell in 2025 because income outran costs.

For a bank growing this fast, the useful question is how to take on the next fifth of business without the next fifth of people. That is capacity, and it is the first thing agents give a lean bank: those four kinds of work handled at volumes that no longer set the size of the team.

The rate tailwind is fading

Part of the low ratio was earned on the income side. In 2025 the ten largest banks’ net interest margin fell 19 basis points to 2.48% as rates came down, and net interest income grew 6.7%, well below the growth in the balance sheet. The dirham is pegged to the dollar, so UAE rates follow the US Federal Reserve’s.

When income growth slows towards cost growth, a low ratio stops falling and can start to rise. A bank that keeps adding cost at today’s pace while its income growth slows will watch 28% drift back up.

Agents protect the ratio from the cost side: if costs stop following volumes, the ratio holds even when margins don’t. The income side is where assist work comes in. Bankers who spend less time on preparation and paperwork can cover more clients, price better and answer faster, and that is income a lean bank needs when each loan earns a little less. A lean bank can’t count on rates, so the lower-rate paths are the ones to plan for.

Deposits that can move

Much of any bank’s profit comes from deposits that cost it little. At the UAE’s ten largest banks, current and savings account balances grew 7.6% in the first quarter of 2025, faster than deposits as a whole at 5.8% (Alvarez & Marsal).

These are the balances the customer agents described in section 04 will look at first. Some will move on rate whatever the bank does. The rest stays for other reasons: salaries and payments run through the account, the bank answers quickly, and its own agents can deal with a customer’s agent without friction. That is respond work, and for a lean bank it is defence as much as service.

Measuring it with jaws

Banks already have the measure for this. They call it jaws: income growth minus cost growth. In 2025 the ten largest UAE banks had jaws of about three points, 10.6% income growth against about 7.5% cost growth. A lean bank’s agent programme should be judged on whether it keeps those jaws open as margins fall, rather than on how much cost it removes.

A bank at 55% can still find savings the old way. A bank at 28% has less room for that, so its case for agents rests on growth: a ratio that holds while the bank expands and margins fall, and deposits that stay.

07Who pays when the work crosses teams

Whether a bank starts at 55% or 28%, the cost it avoids and the capacity it gains have to be counted somewhere, and shared work makes that hard. Follow the letter of credit through a bank today and its cost lands in at least four places. Trade operations checks the documents, financial crime screens the parties and the route, client service answers the client’s questions, and the relationship team handles the exception. Each team’s cost sits in its own cost centre and reaches the trade business through an allocation key, usually headcount or volume. Nobody sees what a letter of credit actually costs from end to end.

Shared agents make it harder still to see. One document-reading agent may serve trade, lending, onboarding and finance, and the platform beneath it serves everyone. The investment appears in the technology budget, the savings appear in operations cost centres, and every team the agent touched claims the benefit. If cost is assigned the old way, the bank will overstate its savings, its technology spending will look like a problem, and business heads will have little reason to push for more autonomy, because they never see the unit cost of the work they consume.

The answer is to price the work rather than the teams. Each kind of work gets a published unit price: per document captured, per alert triaged, per payment repaired, per request answered. The price is fully loaded, covering compute, a share of the platform, and the minutes of human review the case still needs. Business units are charged for the units they use, so the cost of a letter of credit becomes the sum of the units it consumed, wherever in the bank they were produced.

A few rules keep this honest. Savings are booked once, in the business that buys the work, as the change in unit price multiplied by volume against the baseline; operations and technology are measured on unit cost and quality instead. The platform is funded centrally for the first year or two, while unit costs are still coming down from launch levels, and then moves to charging by use on a published schedule, so early adopters aren’t charged for everyone else’s learning. Because review minutes are priced into each unit, a business can see what the judgement floor costs it. That gives it a reason to ask risk for a higher trust level, and gives risk the evidence to decide.

08What changes for the wider system

Competition intensifies once the cost of switching falls to the cost of an agent’s request, and margins on simple products narrow as money moves to wherever it earns most.

Supervisors are likely to expect more. Assurance today largely means sampling files after the event. With agents every case can be checked as it happens and the evidence kept, and regulators will want clear answers on who is accountable when an agent acts.

There are new sources of systemic risk. If most banks’ agents depend on a small number of model providers, one fault or outage could affect many institutions at the same time. And when a customer’s agent deals with a bank’s agent, each needs a reliable way to establish who it is acting for and what it is allowed to do.

Training is a longer-term concern. The junior work through which credit officers and relationship managers learned their trade is the first work agents take on. Without new routes to experience, the industry will eventually have fewer people capable of the judgement it depends on.

Some effects are plainly positive. Trade documents on busy corridors could clear in hours, and small businesses that were too expensive to serve well become worth serving.

09How to know it’s working

The benefits are hard to prove, because the work changes while the business keeps growing. Six practices help.

  1. Measure time, volume, cost and quality for each kind of work in each business before agents are introduced, so there is a baseline to compare against.
  2. Track cost per unit of work using the same unit prices the businesses are charged, along with cycle time and first-time-right rates for customers and revenue per banker for assist work. The number of agents deployed tells you very little.
  3. Introduce agents in stages and compare results with teams that have not yet switched, to separate their effect from market growth.
  4. Record capacity released separately from value realised. Capacity becomes value only when it shows up in the accounts: as cost removed, as revenue earned, or as cost avoided against a baseline finance agreed in advance, such as the headcount a business plan would have needed at the new volume.
  5. Monitor errors, complaints, control breaches and model-risk findings alongside the savings, and don’t count savings that came from taking on more risk.
  6. Track jaws and incremental cost-to-income: the cost added for each unit of new income. At a lean bank these matter more than unit cost. In 2025 the UAE’s ten largest banks added about 21 fils of cost for each new dirham of income, below their 28% average, which is why their ratio fell. When that number climbs above the average, the ratio starts to rise.

Finance should own the record of realised value, built from the same unit prices that appear in the business units’ costs, so the forecast and the cost lines come from one set of numbers. Together, the unit costs and the record of realised value answer the questions a board will ask: whether customers are better served, whether revenue has grown, whether each unit of work costs less, and whether the bank is as safe as before.

10How to organise around it

Once work is priced by the unit, the organisation follows fairly naturally. Business units own clients, journeys and the profit and loss. They decide how much of each kind of work to buy, and they keep the judgement, the relationships and the risk decisions that make up their product. Each kind of work has one owner across the bank, who runs its agents and the people who supervise them, publishes its unit price and answers for its quality. A small catalogue of reusable agents covers the six kinds, each built once and given different context in each business.

One platform holds what everything else depends on: the bank’s knowledge and memory, agent identities, controls, evidence and compute. Decision rights matter as much as the boxes. The business decides whether and where to use a kind of work. The work owner decides how it is done. Risk decides how much autonomy each use is allowed, and can withdraw it. The platform sets standards no business can lower. When a journey crosses teams, as a letter of credit does, the business that owns the client owns the journey’s end-to-end cost and cycle time and buys units from the work owners.

Two simpler designs are tempting. Turning operations into six work factories, each with its own profit and loss, loses sight of the customer. Letting each business build its own agents recreates today’s fragmentation, with many document readers, each trained on part of the bank’s knowledge and none of its memory. The matrix is harder to run, but it is the only one of the three in which both the customer and the unit cost have an owner.

Outside operations, jobs change more than they disappear. Bankers get time back for clients, analysts decide instead of drafting, and the operations staff who stay move from processing cases to supervising agents and handling exceptions. New roles appear around the memory and the platform: people who keep the bank’s knowledge accurate, who watch agents in production, and who own each kind of work. Those roles need career paths before the old ones shrink.

11 · ActionWhere to start

The first areas to hand to agents are those with high volumes, standardised steps and errors that can be reversed. Decisions with larger consequences come next, with a person making the final call. Where the judgement itself is what the customer pays for, agents should support people rather than act on their own.

A lean bank should order the list by growth. In the first quadrant, start with the capture and process work that grows fastest with the balance sheet: documents and KYC for new clients, payments and trade documents. Alert triage, which rises with transactions, comes next, with a person deciding. Assist and respond follow where speed turns into revenue and keeps clients: a credit case prepared the same day for a banker to decide, a cross-border payment that clears in hours, a query answered in minutes.

A sensible first quarter has three steps. Measure how one business spends its time across the six kinds of work, which takes a few weeks and replaces the assumptions here with real data. Appoint an owner for the bank’s knowledge and case memory, with rules on what is kept and who can use it. Then choose two high-volume, low-risk areas, set a unit price for each, and measure it before and after.

12What leaders have to decide

All banks can buy similar models at falling prices, and as I argued in The Capability–Value Gap, the advantage moves to the system around the model. In a bank, that system is set by a few early decisions: whether, given where the bank starts, agents are a cost programme or a growth one; which decisions remain with people, and how the bank keeps developing people able to make them; who owns the bank’s knowledge and the memory its agents build; how much autonomy agents are given, on what evidence and with whom accountable; and how shared work is priced, so that savings are real and land where they can be banked.

I expect customers to see the difference well before it shows up in the financial results.
