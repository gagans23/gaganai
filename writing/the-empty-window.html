The Empty Window

Context windows grew five thousandfold in six years. The context an enterprise can actually supply did not move at all. That gap — not model capability — is the next decade of enterprise AI.

Gagan Sachdeva · August 2026 · ~16 min read · Enterprise AI · Context & Knowledge

A few weeks ago I sat through a demo of a genuinely good agent, built by a genuinely good team. Million-token window. Loaded with the policy manual, the product catalogue, three years of tickets, and the complete customer file. Every question we put to it came back correct.

Then someone asked why a particular exception had been approved back in March.

The agent produced the approval record. Amount, approver, timestamp, the policy clause cited. All accurate. All useless. Nobody had asked what happened. They had asked why anyone thought it was a reasonable thing to do.

The person who knew was sitting four seats down. She answered in about eleven seconds — something about that counterparty, a restructuring two years earlier, and a pattern in how their treasury team files things at quarter end. Nothing she said existed in any of the systems we had just poured into that window.

We have spent six years making the window bigger. We have spent almost none of that effort on what we put in it.

The argument in 60 seconds

Context windows have scaled roughly five thousandfold since 2020, and the frontier has converged near a million input tokens. But the usable window is materially smaller than the advertised one, and — more importantly — almost no institution is limited by it. The binding constraint is supply: the reasoning behind enterprise decisions is mostly undocumented, held in the heads of a small number of long-tenured operators. Model scaling is a compute problem. Context scaling, in an enterprise, is an institutional knowledge problem. The window is rented and abundant. The supply is yours, scarce, and non-transferable — which is precisely why it is the moat.

01 · SignalFive Thousand Times Bigger

The numbers are worth putting in one place, because we have normalised them so quickly that the shape of the curve has stopped registering.

GPT-3 shipped in 2020 with 2,048 tokens — about four pages. In May 2023 Anthropic put out the first 100K-token window from a frontier lab; by that November, 200K. In February 2024 Gemini 1.5 Pro crossed a million, and reached two million by May. In April 2025 Llama 4 Scout claimed ten million. By this year the closed frontier has quietly converged: OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and Amazon all sit around a million input tokens, with output caps an order of magnitude below that.

Four pages to roughly a million tokens in six years. Somewhere between three and four orders of magnitude, depending on where you draw the line, and the curve has not obviously stopped.

Everyone read this as a capability story. I think it is closer to an appetite story. The window is now comfortably larger than what most institutions can honestly fill.

02The Window Has a Second, Quieter Number

A caveat on the demand side first, because otherwise someone will write to me about it. The advertised window is not the working window.

The RULER benchmark, which tests retrieval, multi-key lookup and pattern matching at increasing lengths, finds degradation as input grows across every model tested. A Chroma study across eighteen models found all eighteen degraded — and, counterintuitively, that models often did better on shuffled text than on coherent passages, because coherent prose sets up positional patterns that trigger recency bias. The older “lost in the middle” result still holds: material placed in the centre of a long context is harder to recover than material at either end.

The practical planning number that has settled out of this work is roughly sixty to seventy per cent of advertised capacity, and performance tends to hold and then fall off a cliff rather than decline gracefully. A model advertising a million tokens can start losing the thread somewhere north of fifty thousand, depending on what you put in front of it.

This is a real constraint and the people working on it are not wasting their time. I just think it is ranked too highly. In most of the enterprises I see, it is a second-order problem, because nobody is straining these windows with too much good material. They are straining them with the same policy PDF four times, once per source system.

Almost no enterprise is context-limited by the model. They are context-limited by themselves.

03 · MeaningDocuments Are the Residue, Not the Knowledge

Look at what actually goes into an enterprise context window. Policy documents. Wiki and Confluence pages. Ticket histories. CRM notes. Warehouse extracts. Meeting transcripts, if you are ambitious.

Every one of those is the output of a decision process. None of them is the process.

A credit memo records that the exposure was approved at a certain level with certain conditions. It does not record the four things the underwriter checked and dismissed in the first ten minutes, the line in the management accounts that made her uneasy, why she pushed back on the model score, or what she would have done differently had the request landed three weeks later. The memo is the residue. The reasoning evaporated, and it evaporated on purpose, because writing it all down would have taken longer than the decision.

IDC put the problem well recently: organisations do not know how much tacit knowledge they have, and estimates of the undocumented share range anywhere from a quarter to ninety per cent. That range is not a weakness in the research. It is the finding. We are talking about firms that can tell you their capital ratio to two decimal places and cannot tell you, even to the nearest order of magnitude, what fraction of their operating knowledge is written down anywhere.

The adjacent numbers rhyme. Eighty-nine per cent of organisations acknowledge a data quality problem. Fifty-two per cent call data quality the single most important factor in AI success. Seven in ten leaders name data silos as a major barrier. More than half of AI initiatives stall after the pilot. These are self-reported surveys and should be read as directional rather than precise — but I have never met an operator who found the direction surprising.

04The Knowledge Sits in About Forty People

The phrase “institutional knowledge” is a category error, and I think it does real damage. It makes the knowledge sound like it lives in the institution. It does not. In most large firms it lives in a startlingly small number of people, and if you ask around for twenty minutes you can usually name them.

Take the ones you could name in any large bank inside twenty minutes.

A note on the examples that follow. They are drawn from public rulebooks, published trade agreements and how this industry works in general — not from any institution I have worked with. Nothing here describes an employer’s systems, clients or live problems. The point is the shape of the problem, which is common to every bank operating these corridors.

The clearest case I know is trade finance document checking, because it is the one where the automation genuinely works and the gap is still total.

Take the UAE–India corridor, which is one of the busiest documentary trade routes in the world and, since the two countries brought a comprehensive economic partnership agreement into force in 2022, one of the most procedurally interesting. A presentation arrives under a letter of credit: commercial invoice, bill of lading, packing list, certificate of origin, insurance certificate, draft. A checker examines them against UCP 600 and has five banking days to decide.

This is the most codified corner of banking. Thirty-nine articles, decades of ICC opinions, a rulebook the whole industry has agreed to. If any part of the work were going to automate cleanly it is this part, and it does. Document intelligence pulls the fields. A rules engine checks them: is the presentation inside the credit validity and within twenty-one days of shipment, does the invoice description correspond to the credit, is the insurance at least a hundred and ten per cent of CIF, is the bill of lading properly signed and endorsed. That layer works now. It is faster and more consistent than a person, and it does not get tired at four in the afternoon.

Then it produces its answer, and the answer is discrepant. It usually is. Industry estimates put refusal on first presentation somewhere between sixty and seventy-five per cent, with wide regional variation.

Three times in four, the machine finishes its job and hands over a word that is not a decision. It is the beginning of one.

Now add the structure that makes this corridor its own discipline. Under a preferential trade agreement, the certificate of origin stops being a formality and becomes the document that carries the money — it is what determines whether the goods clear at the preferential tariff or the ordinary one. So an origin discrepancy is not a documentary irritation. It is a duty exposure, and whose exposure it is depends on the incoterm and on what the underlying contract says. Layer on the re-export reality of the region — goods arriving, being consolidated and moving on again — and questions of origin, transshipment and sequence of documents stop being clerical and start being commercial.

None of that is in the rules engine, because none of it is a rule. It is a set of judgements, and they are the entire job:

  • Is this a discrepancy the issuing bank will actually refuse, or one it has never once refused in this trade lane?
  • Is it worth raising at all, or faster to have the beneficiary correct and re-present inside the credit validity?
  • Will the applicant waive? Some always do, some never do, and the difference is not written anywhere.
  • Is the discrepancy technically valid and commercially meaningless — a category that exists constantly and appears in no rulebook?
  • Does this one touch the tariff preference, in which case it stops being a documents question and becomes a customs one?
  • And if we refuse: who tells the relationship manager before the client does?

Two checkers, same rulebook, same documents, same automation. Materially different outcomes for the client — paid this week or paid next month, and in trade that difference is the working capital line. The gap between them is not competence and it is not rule knowledge. It is years of watching how a particular lane behaves.

So the bank automates the extraction and the rules, which is the written half, and reports a productivity gain, which is real. What it has actually done is produce the word discrepant faster, and hand it to the same person who was always the constraint — because the thing she does was never the rule-checking. The rules were never the bottleneck. They were the only part written down.

The same shape repeats wherever the rules are written and the judgement is not. An analyst works a financial crime alert queue on one of the Gulf’s remittance corridors — the region moves an enormous volume of small, regular, entirely legitimate transfers to South Asia — and knows the queue swells predictably around particular festivals and pay cycles, that almost all of it is ordinary remittance behaviour rather than structuring, and that exactly one pattern inside that swell is worth escalating. The model that raised the alerts does not know this. The case notes record the disposition, not the reasoning. When that analyst moves teams, the false-positive rate on that corridor drifts up for a quarter and nobody can say precisely why.

A cross-border payment fails on a correspondent’s format validation. The operations lead knows which field that correspondent actually enforces as opposed to which fields its published specification says it enforces, and what the repair is. Correspondent networks are held together by this kind of knowledge. It appears in no runbook, partly because writing it down would mean formally admitting the workaround exists.

A credit committee reviews a regional real estate exposure and overrides the model score. The minutes record the override, the conditions and the vote. They do not record the two questions someone asked in the room that changed three people’s minds — usually about a developer’s delivery record or how a structure behaved in the last cycle. Those questions are the institution’s actual credit policy. The written policy is what the institution says it does.

Every one of those is an AI use case that somebody has already put on a slide, usually with a percentage next to it. Every one of them fails in the same place, and it is not the model. It is the point where the model needs the thing that was never written down.

None of it is written down. Not because these people are careless; they tend to be the most conscientious people in the building. It is that the knowledge is not writable in the form it exists in. It is pattern, sequence, exception handling, and a sense built over years of what usually goes wrong. Polanyi’s observation that we know more than we can tell was a point about epistemology. It has quietly become a procurement question.

And here is the part that should hold a board’s attention: this knowledge is most concentrated exactly where the workforce is thinnest, oldest, and closest to retirement. The worker bee is not a bottleneck to be automated around. For the moment, she is the knowledge layer.

You are not deploying AI into an information-rich enterprise. You are deploying it into a documented enterprise sitting on top of a much larger undocumented one.

05The Scaling Law That Actually Applies

Model scaling laws relate loss to compute, data and parameters. They are clean because every term is measurable and every term is buyable.

Context growth is being read as the same kind of curve — bigger window, better system, keep going. It is not, and the reason is structural. The window is a container. Containers do not scale value on their own. What scales enterprise value is what the institution can put in the container truthfully, at the moment the decision is being made.

So the version I would write down is this. The value of an agentic system scales with the institutional context it can admit per unit of model attention — not with the size of the window, and not with the size of the model.

This is the same argument I have made before in a narrower form: the value of a deployed AI system converges to the quality of the knowledge layer beneath it, not the model within it. Context scaling sharpens it rather than replacing it. When the window was eight thousand tokens, the knowledge layer was a nice idea you could defer. At a million tokens, the model layer is abundant, commoditised and rentable, and the supply layer is the only term left that differs between you and your competitor.

Which is exactly what makes it a moat rather than a cost. Anyone can rent a million-token window this afternoon. Nobody can rent thirty years of knowing which exceptions matter.

06Capturing Supply Without Building a Surveillance Log

The obvious response is “then let us document the tacit knowledge,” and it is also the response that has been failing since the mid-nineties. Knowledge management as a discipline was built on interviews, templates and wikis. What it produced was a library of what people believed they did. The gap between that and what they actually do is the original problem, restated at greater expense.

The more promising direction in the 2024–2026 literature is to stop asking and start observing. Not observing people — observing work: what was attended to, in what sequence, which artefacts were touched, what was abandoned, what was resumed and with what.

  • The X-SYNTH work proposes digital twin signatures and attention-weighted retrieval, synthesising enterprise context from observed digital attention rather than from documents alone.
  • A-Mem argues that agent memory should link and evolve as new experience arrives, instead of accumulating as an append-only log.
  • The context engineering survey reframes all of this as a pipeline — retrieval, processing, management, memory, tool reasoning — rather than a retrieval trick bolted onto a prompt.
  • Agentic context engineering shows contexts can be curated as evolving playbooks without collapsing into summary mush.
  • The agent-native memory work decomposes the whole thing into representation, extraction, retrieval and maintenance — four modules that should be operable and inspectable, not hidden backend internals.

The same literature carries two warnings that belong in any board paper on this. Digital Twins as Funhouse Mirrors finds that LLM-based human twins can correlate weakly with the actual humans they model and distort systematically. Trust calibration research on twin agents identifies schema, epistemic and model-artefact gaps that appear the moment an agent starts speaking as a person. Neither is a reason not to build. Both are reasons to put confidence, evidence age, missing context and applied privacy gates on the surface of every answer, where a user can see them.

And the collection has to be governed at the point of capture, not at the point of query. Foreground application, redacted window title, dwell time, derived work domain, switching sequence — yes. Keystrokes, clipboard, microphone, camera, raw screenshots — no, and not as a configurable default either. Redact before storage, keep it local, export summaries rather than events. This is not only a privacy position. It is an accuracy position: a worker who believes they are being logged will change what they do, and you will have captured the performance rather than the practice.

I have been building a small local-first version of this to think the problem through properly, rather than argue it in the abstract — a digital twin sensor that observes attention on one machine, redacts before it stores, builds a context graph and working spheres, and exports summary-only context packs through a visible admission gate. The code is open source and on GitHub; the case study walks through the mechanism.

Building it taught me something I had been saying without quite believing. For a while the repository had a respectable set of unit tests and no way at all to answer the only question that matters: is the context this thing hands an agent any good? Relevant enough to be useful, tight enough to be safe. Those are measurements, and I had not built the instrument. So I wrote one — a golden set of scenarios, scored for recall, for noise, and for whether a deliberately planted piece of fake card data ever reaches an export.

It failed on its first run, and it failed on something real. Evidence sixty days old was being handed out inside a three-day window, because the window was enforced at the database and merely recorded everywhere after it. Every unit test passed. They had been passing for months. That is the whole argument in miniature: the tests were checking that the code did what it said, and the thing that was wrong was what it said.

07What Happens When It Spreads

Everything to this point I am fairly confident about. What follows is speculation, and I would rather label it than dress it up.

Suppose the supply problem gets solved — not perfectly, just well enough that a firm can see how its own work is actually done. The interesting property of that capability is that it does not scale linearly. It changes kind at each tier. Every level up, context appears that was invisible from the level below, and with it a set of things that start running without anyone asking for them.

At the level of one person it is a productivity toy: your agent starts the morning knowing which of three threads went cold at four o’clock yesterday. At the level of a team it stops being a toy, because the seam between people becomes visible — who actually holds which expertise, as opposed to who holds which title, and what falls in the gap at handover. That is also the first point at which the ethics stop being theoretical. Ten people can identify each other from anonymised data without trying.

A department gets something genuinely new: the process as practised, side by side with the process as written. I have never seen those two documents match. A business unit sees the shadow operating model — the routes work actually takes between functions, where it stalls, and which controls people quietly route around, along with the reason they route around them. That last one is the finding that gets a programme cancelled, which is why a system that only surfaces comfortable results is worthless.

At the level of a whole organisation the thing you get is institutional memory as a maintained asset rather than a departure risk — and agents grounded in how this firm decides, not how a model assumes firms in general decide. That is the entire argument of this essay, arriving as an operational capability rather than a thesis.

Above the firm it gets more speculative and more consequential. A sector could benchmark how work is done rather than only what it produced, federated, with no raw data leaving any participant — and a regulator could finally see the distance between what a rule says and what supervised firms actually do. A country could observe where skills genuinely sit, rather than inferring it from job titles on a census, and measure the effect of a policy change on real work in weeks instead of surveying it in years.

And at the far end there is a map of how human work is actually done, kept current: the thing every frontier lab is currently trying to approximate from text, available directly.

The scarce resource was never intelligence. It is legible practice — a truthful account of how work gets done, in a form a machine can use.

I want to be careful with the last two rungs, because this is exactly where writing of this kind usually turns triumphant and stops being useful.

The technical path from one sensor to a billion is not hard. It is packaging, enrolment, policy distribution and a summary-sync protocol — all solved problems in endpoint management, none of them research. Somebody is going to build it. The only genuine question is whether the version that wins has a gate in it: redaction before the write, aggregation floors, a visible not-captured ledger, summary-only export, a delete that actually deletes. Those are cheap to build at the first tier and effectively impossible to retrofit at the sixth. The governance has to be in the first commit or it never arrives at all.

Which is the part I find genuinely uncomfortable. Everything above the organisation tier stops being a company’s decision and becomes a question for a society, and societies are slow at questions like this. The engineering will be finished long before the consent architecture is. That asymmetry, not the capability itself, is what I would worry about.

08 · ActionStart Monday

None of this requires a new platform, a new vendor or a new budget cycle. It requires asking a different question than the one currently on the slide.

Five moves
  • Measure supply, not capacity. Take one workflow. For last quarter’s decisions, what fraction of the reasoning exists anywhere retrievable? For most firms this is the first honest number a board has ever seen on the subject, and it is usually shocking.
  • Stop buying window you cannot fill. Effective context is well under advertised, and paying million-token rates to send forty thousand tokens of duplicated PDF is a line item, not a strategy.
  • Name the forty people. Literally write the list. Which roles hold knowledge that would take eighteen months to rebuild if they left this year? That list is a knowledge risk register, and it belongs next to your operational risk register.
  • Capture by observation, under a gate. Attention and sequence, redacted before storage, summary-only on export, with the gate and its exclusions visible to the person being observed. Consent that cannot be inspected is not consent.
  • Make every agent show its supply. Confidence, evidence age, source artefacts, and what was withheld and why. An agent that cannot tell you what it did not have cannot be trusted with what it did.
  • Then measure the supply, continuously. A golden set of real questions, scored for whether the right context surfaced and whether anything surfaced that should not have. Run it on every change, like a test suite. Nobody does this yet, which is why so many pilots degrade quietly for a quarter before anyone notices.

The woman four seats down answered that question in about eleven seconds. She was not faster than the model. She had something the model was never given, and no amount of extra window was going to fix that.

The window stopped being the constraint some time in 2024. What we do with the next five thousandfold depends entirely on whether we have anything true to put in it.
