The Empty Window

Context windows grew five thousandfold in six years. The context an enterprise can actually supply did not move at all. That gap — not model capability — is the next decade of enterprise AI.

Gagan Sachdeva · August 2026 · ~17 min read · Enterprise AI · Context & Knowledge

A few weeks ago I sat through a demo of a genuinely good agent, built by a genuinely good team. Million-token window. Loaded with the policy manual, the product catalogue, three years of tickets, and the complete customer file. Every question we put to it came back correct.

Then someone asked why a particular exception had been approved back in March.

The agent produced the approval record. Amount, approver, timestamp, the policy clause cited. All accurate. All useless. Nobody had asked what happened. They had asked why anyone thought it was a reasonable thing to do.

The person who knew was sitting four seats down. She answered in about eleven seconds, and her answer had three parts: something about that counterparty, something about what had happened the last time we saw this shape of request, and something about who had been in the room. Nothing she said existed in any of the systems we had just poured into that window.

I should be honest about where I stand, because it is why I find this interesting rather than merely true. I have spent years running and reviewing enterprise AI programmes. I have signed off on work that automated the written half of a process, reported the productivity gain, and called it finished. The gain was real. The finishing was not. What follows is partly an argument and partly an account of a mistake I made repeatedly before I could name it.

We have spent six years making the window bigger. We have spent almost none of that effort on what we put in it.

The argument in 60 seconds

Context windows have scaled roughly five thousandfold since 2020, and the frontier has converged near a million input tokens. But the usable window is materially smaller than the advertised one, and — more importantly — almost no institution is limited by it. The binding constraint is supply: the reasoning behind enterprise decisions is mostly undocumented, held in the heads of a small number of long-tenured operators. Model scaling is a compute problem. Context scaling, in an enterprise, is an institutional knowledge problem. The window is rented and abundant. The supply is yours, scarce, and non-transferable — which is precisely why it is the moat.

01 · SignalFive Thousand Times Bigger

The numbers are worth putting in one place, because we have normalised them so quickly that the shape of the curve has stopped registering.

GPT-3 shipped in 2020 with 2,048 tokens — about four pages. In May 2023 Anthropic put out the first 100K-token window from a frontier lab; by that November, 200K. In February 2024 Gemini 1.5 Pro crossed a million, and reached two million by May. In April 2025 Llama 4 Scout claimed ten million. By this year the closed frontier has quietly converged: OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and Amazon all sit around a million input tokens, with output caps an order of magnitude below that.

Four pages to roughly a million tokens in six years. Somewhere between three and four orders of magnitude, depending on where you draw the line, and the curve has not obviously stopped.

Everyone read this as a capability story. I think it is closer to an appetite story. The window is now comfortably larger than what most institutions can honestly fill.

One caveat on the demand side, because otherwise someone will write to me about it. The advertised window is not the working window. The RULER benchmark and a Chroma study across eighteen models both find retrieval degrading as input grows, and the practical planning number that has settled out of that work is roughly sixty to seventy per cent of advertised capacity — holding, then falling off a cliff rather than declining gracefully. This is a real constraint and the people working on it are not wasting their time. I just think it is ranked too highly, because in most of the enterprises I see nobody is straining these windows with too much good material. They are straining them with the same policy PDF four times, once per source system.

Almost no enterprise is context-limited by the model. They are context-limited by themselves.

02 · MeaningThe Missing Half

Look at what actually goes into an enterprise context window. Policy documents. Wiki and Confluence pages. Ticket histories. CRM notes. Warehouse extracts. Meeting transcripts, if you are ambitious.

Every one of those is the output of a decision process. None of them is the process.

A credit memo records that the exposure was approved at a certain level with certain conditions. It does not record the four things the underwriter checked and dismissed in the first ten minutes, the line in the management accounts that made her uneasy, why she pushed back on the model score, or what she would have done differently had the request landed three weeks later. The memo is the residue. The reasoning evaporated, and it evaporated on purpose, because writing it all down would have taken longer than the decision.

IDC put the problem well recently: organisations do not know how much tacit knowledge they have, and estimates of the undocumented share range anywhere from a quarter to ninety per cent. That range is not a weakness in the research. It is the finding. We are talking about firms that can tell you their capital ratio to two decimal places and cannot tell you, even to the nearest order of magnitude, what fraction of their operating knowledge is written down anywhere.

The adjacent numbers rhyme. Eighty-nine per cent of organisations acknowledge a data quality problem. Fifty-two per cent call data quality the single most important factor in AI success. Seven in ten leaders name data silos as a major barrier. More than half of AI initiatives stall after the pilot. These are self-reported surveys and should be read as directional rather than precise — but I have never met an operator who found the direction surprising.

The phrase “institutional knowledge” is a category error, and I think it does real damage. It makes the knowledge sound like it lives in the institution. It does not. In most large firms it lives in a startlingly small number of people, and if you ask around for twenty minutes you can usually name them.

Take the ones you could name in any large bank inside twenty minutes.

A note on the examples that follow. They are drawn from public rulebooks, published trade agreements and how this industry works in general — not from any institution I have worked with. Nothing here describes an employer’s systems, clients or live problems. The point is the shape of the problem, which is common to every bank operating these corridors.

The clearest case I know is trade finance document checking, because it is the one where the automation genuinely works and the gap is still total.

A presentation arrives under a letter of credit — invoice, bill of lading, packing list, certificate of origin, insurance certificate, draft. A checker examines them against UCP 600 and has five banking days to decide. This is the most codified corner of banking: thirty-nine articles and decades of ICC opinions. If any part of the work were going to automate cleanly it is this, and it does. Document intelligence pulls the fields, a rules engine checks them against the credit, and it is faster and more consistent than a person.

Then it produces its answer, and the answer is discrepant. It usually is — industry estimates put refusal on first presentation between sixty and seventy-five per cent.

Three times in four, the machine finishes its job and hands over a word that is not a decision. It is the beginning of one.

Everything after that word is judgement, and none of it is written down. Will this issuing bank actually refuse, or has it never once refused this in this lane? Faster to raise it, or to have the beneficiary correct and re-present inside the credit validity? Will the applicant waive — some always do, some never, and the difference is nowhere? Is it technically valid and commercially meaningless, a category that exists constantly and appears in no rulebook? On the UAE–India corridor there is a further turn: under a preferential trade agreement the certificate of origin determines duty treatment, so an origin discrepancy stops being a documents question and becomes a customs exposure.

Two checkers, same rulebook, same documents, same automation, materially different outcomes for the client — and in trade that difference is the working capital line. So the bank automates the extraction and the rules, reports a real productivity gain, and has in fact produced the word discrepant faster and handed it to the same person who was always the constraint. The rules were never the bottleneck. They were the only part written down.

The same shape repeats wherever the rules are written and the judgement is not. The financial crime analyst on a Gulf remittance corridor who knows which festival swell is ordinary behaviour and which single pattern inside it is worth escalating. The payments lead who knows which field a correspondent actually enforces, as opposed to what its published specification says. The credit committee whose minutes record the override, the conditions and the vote, but not the two questions asked in the room that changed three people’s minds. Each of these is an AI use case already on somebody’s slide, usually with a percentage next to it, and each fails at the same point: where the model needs the thing that was never written down. The record keeps the decision and loses the reasoning.

None of it is written down. Not because these people are careless; they tend to be the most conscientious people in the building. It is that the knowledge is not writable in the form it exists in. It is pattern, sequence, exception handling, and a sense built over years of what usually goes wrong. Polanyi’s observation that we know more than we can tell was a point about epistemology. It has quietly become a procurement question.

And here is the part that should hold a board’s attention: this knowledge is most concentrated exactly where the workforce is thinnest, oldest, and closest to retirement. The worker bee is not a bottleneck to be automated around. For the moment, she is the knowledge layer.

You are not deploying AI into an information-rich enterprise. You are deploying it into a documented enterprise sitting on top of a much larger undocumented one.

03 · ExperimentI Built It Over a Weekend to Find Out What I Thought

Everything above I had been saying for a while. I was not sure I believed it.

There is a particular discomfort in making an argument about instrumentation while having built no instrument. So over a weekend I sat down with about ten papers open and started writing code — fast, loose, the way you do when the point is not the artefact but finding out where your own reasoning breaks. Vibe coding, if you like, except the vibe was a stack of arXiv tabs and a growing suspicion that I had been wrong about which part was hard.

What came out is a sensor that watches attention rather than content, redacts before it stores, builds a graph of what was worked on and in what order, and exports summary-only context packs through a gate that has to approve every field. It is open source. The case study has the mechanism in full.

But the code is not the interesting output. The interesting output is that building it changed my mind three times, and I could not have got there by reading.

Reading and building are different activities

I had read the literature properly. X-SYNTH on synthesising context from observed attention. A-Mem on memory that links and evolves. The context engineering survey framing all of it as a pipeline. Agent-native memory decomposing it into representation, extraction, retrieval, maintenance.

Reading them, I came away with a shopping list of capabilities. Implementing them, I discovered the two that mattered most were the ones proposing no capability at all. Digital Twins as Funhouse Mirrors shows that LLM-based human twins correlate weakly with the humans they model and distort systematically. Trust calibration work identifies the gaps that open the moment an agent speaks as someone. Neither tells you what to build. Both tell you what not to claim — and in this domain that turns out to be the harder engineering constraint. It is why the thing is called a sensor and not a twin.

Four of the ten changed the code. Two changed it partially. Four are read, understood, roadmapped and not built. Saying which is which seems more useful than implying otherwise.

The first thing I was wrong about

I expected the difficulty to be in observing work. It is not. Watching which application has focus, for how long, in what order, is a weekend problem.

Every genuinely hard decision turned out to be a refusal. Not what to collect — what to decline. Browser domain yes, query string never, and those had to be separate switches because the domain tells you the workstream and the query tells you far more than anyone agreed to. Redaction before the write rather than before the query. The export re-masking even though the store is already masked.

None of that is intellectually hard. All of it is easy to skip, and skipping it produces a system that works better and is worth less, because nobody will run it.

The second thing, which is the one that unsettled me

By Sunday the repository had a respectable set of unit tests, all passing. And I had no way to answer the only question that matters: is the context this hands an agent any good? Useful enough to matter, tight enough to be safe. Those are measurements, and I had not built the instrument — which is precisely the failure I had spent five sections accusing everyone else of.

So I wrote a harness: real scenarios, scored for whether the right context surfaced, how much noise came with it, and whether a deliberately planted piece of fake card data ever reached an export.

It failed on its first run, on something real. Evidence sixty days old was being handed out inside a three-day window, because the window was enforced at the database and merely recorded everywhere after it. Twenty-two unit tests had been green for weeks. They were checking that the code did what it said. The thing that was wrong was what it said.

Unit tests verify implementation against intent. Only a harness verifies intent against reality — and those fail in completely different ways.

The third thing, and where my thinking on scaling actually changed

Then it got recursive, and this is the part I would not have reached by reasoning about it in the abstract.

The harness only checks what I already knew to plant. It proves a known string did not escape. It cannot tell me whether someone could resume the work from a pack, or what an adversary might infer from one that leaked nothing literal. So I added judges — adversarial reviewers whose job is to try to re-identify a person from a supposedly safe export.

And those are opinions. To know whether they are right, the system needs the person whose work it is to say useful, wrong, stale, too broad, too private, missing context. Which is a layer, and that layer needs judging too.

This is where my thinking about scaling actually moved, and it moved somewhere less comfortable than where it started.

I went into the weekend with a tidy claim in my pocket. I had been describing context supply as a law, the way capacity has one — as if some clean relationship connected operators observed to context produced. Building the thing cured me of the word. There is a relationship, but nothing in that sentence has a unit yet. Nobody has defined the y-axis, let alone measured it, and the honest name for what I hold now is a hypothesis with an instrument attached.

I had been carrying an implicit model in which context supply is a procurement problem: hard, expensive, but the kind of thing a large organisation can buy its way through with enough platform and enough programme. Building the thing killed that model. There is no bottom to the stack. Every layer that judges the one below is itself only as good as a human willing to say that was wrong. Which means the operator is not something the instrument eventually removes from the loop. The instrument exists to give the operator something to correct.

So the scaling story is not "capture more." It is: how cheaply can you get a knowledgeable person to correct a machine's account of their own work — and how much of that correction compounds. That is a very different thing to build, and a very different thing to sell, and I did not see it clearly until the third instrument.

What vibe coding actually taught me

I want to be precise about the method, because "I built it over a weekend" can mean two very different things and only one of them is worth anything.

Building fast with a model is extraordinarily good at finding the shape of a problem and almost useless for telling you whether you are right. By Saturday evening I had a working pipeline, a plausible architecture and a repository that looked like it knew what it was doing. None of that was knowledge. It was structure, produced confidently, at a speed that outran my ability to check it.

Four things I would tell anyone doing this:

Lessons, in the order they cost me something
  • Every shortcut I took was a refusal I skipped. The first version had no admission gate. It was simpler, faster, and worth nothing — because the entire value of the thing is what it declines to do. Speed pushes you toward capability and away from constraint, and constraint was the product.
  • The tests I wrote fast were tests of what I had built, not what I had claimed. Twenty-two of them, all green, none of them checking the thing that was actually broken. Writing tests alongside generated code produces tests that agree with the code. That is a specific and dangerous failure mode.
  • I produced something that looked like a result and was not. A confidence formula, invented in an afternoon, sitting in a repository that carefully cites ten papers. Nobody would have questioned it. Speed makes invented things look inherited.
  • The model was a much better critic than collaborator. The useful moments were not "write me this module." They were "here is what I built, argue against it" — which is also how the adversarial judges in the harness ended up existing at all.

Vibe coding makes the documented half almost free. Which is exactly the asymmetry this essay is about, arriving on my own desk in miniature.

That is the part I did not expect to find. I spent five sections arguing that institutions automate the written half of a process and call it finished. Then I spent a weekend automating the written half of an engineering problem and very nearly called it finished. The gap between a repository that looks correct and one that is correct is the same gap as between a documented enterprise and a real one — and it closes the same way, by somebody knowledgeable checking.

What the weekend answered, and what it did not

Being explicit about this is the difference between an experiment and a demo.

The repository

github.com/gagans23/digital-twin-sensor — MIT, no runtime dependencies, macOS. The collector, the redaction gate, the context graph, the working spheres, the admission gate, the evaluation harness, the adversarial judges, the structured app connectors and the feedback layer are all there, along with the golden set and the honest gaps table.

The two files worth opening first are docs/UNDER_THE_HOOD.md, which explains how attention is defined and what joins the layers, and harness/scenarios.json, which is the golden set — including the planted card number that must never reach an export.

If the mechanism holds, it does not stop at one person. The same capture-and-gate loop stacks — person to team to department to institution — with each tier aggregating the one below under a stricter privacy floor. I have sketched that ladder in the case study rather than argued it here, because it is exactly the kind of extrapolation a weekend cannot earn.

And one thing I got straightforwardly wrong

The synthesis layer scores confidence with a formula. Someone asked me which paper it came from. None — I invented it over that weekend, and it has never been fitted against data, because no labelled data existed yet.

The direction was defensible. The numbers were mine, and sitting in a repository that carefully maps ten papers to design decisions, they would have been read as inherited. That is exactly how an assumption becomes a finding: repeated three times in a credible context until nobody questions it. It is now marked in the code and the docs as an unvalidated prior.

I mention it because it is the same failure mode as this essay. It is very easy to produce the documented half convincingly.

What this does not show

One machine — mine. Never deployed to a team, which is where the aggregation floor either holds or does not. No measured improvement in task-resume time, because that needs a real function with real interruptions over real weeks. The hypotheses are written down and none are tested.

So this is not evidence that the approach works. It is evidence about what building it costs, where the difficulty actually sits, and how far the honest claims are from the confident ones. Less than I wanted. More than I had on Friday.

04Where I Could Be Wrong

The case against all of this comes in two parts, and both deserve to be stated better than their critics usually manage.

The first is that the models may simply not need the supply. Give a strong model the thin context and it infers the rest; that is arguably what these systems are for. And sometimes it works — a good model reconstructs a surprising amount of reasoning from the residue. But inference from residue is precisely how a plausible wrong answer gets produced in banking. The model reconstructs the modal case, and the institution’s value sits in knowing when this case is not the modal one. The forty people are not carrying the average. They are carrying the exceptions.

The second is harder, and I do not have a full answer to it. Observed is not validated. A sensor that captures how the best checker works has captured how she works, not whether she is right — and if you capture her and she leaves, you have frozen an opinion at the moment it stopped being correctable. The judgement layer these instruments feed has to stay attached to people who can still say that was wrong, which is the recursion argument from the experiment arriving from the other direction. Build capture without correction and you have not preserved the institution’s knowledge. You have embalmed it.

05 · ActionStart Monday

None of this requires a new platform, a new vendor or a new budget cycle. It requires asking a different question than the one currently on the slide.

Five moves
  • Measure supply, not capacity — and keep measuring it. Take one workflow. For last quarter’s decisions, what fraction of the reasoning exists anywhere retrievable? For most firms that is the first honest number a board has ever seen on the subject. Then hold it: a golden set of real questions, scored for whether the right context surfaced and whether anything surfaced that should not have, run on every change like a test suite. Nobody does this yet, which is why so many pilots degrade quietly for a quarter before anyone notices.
  • Stop buying window you cannot fill. Effective context is well under advertised, and paying million-token rates to send forty thousand tokens of duplicated PDF is a line item, not a strategy.
  • Name the forty people. Literally write the list. Which roles hold knowledge that would take eighteen months to rebuild if they left this year? That list is a knowledge risk register, and it belongs next to your operational risk register.
  • Capture by observation, under a gate. Attention and sequence, redacted before storage, summary-only on export, with the gate and its exclusions visible to the person being observed. Consent that cannot be inspected is not consent.
  • Make every agent show its supply. Confidence, evidence age, source artefacts, and what was withheld and why. An agent that cannot tell you what it did not have cannot be trusted with what it did.

The woman four seats down answered that question in about eleven seconds. She was not faster than the model. She had something the model was never given, and no amount of extra window was going to fix that.

The window stopped being the constraint some time in 2024. What we do with the next five thousandfold depends entirely on whether we have anything true to put in it.
