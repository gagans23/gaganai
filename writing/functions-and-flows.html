The Philosophical Ledger · Operating models

Enterprises are organised into functions. Work is not.

The org chart was never a map of the work. It was a map of where context could be kept. Agents change what it costs to carry context across a boundary, and that moves where the boundaries belong.

Gagan Sachdeva · 3 October 2026 · ~25 min read · Operating models · Context & knowledge

Follow one piece of work.

A trading company in Dubai imports cotton yarn from a mill in Tamil Nadu. Its bank has issued a letter of credit. The mill ships, the mill’s bank presents the documents, and they reach the issuing bank’s trade operations desk with a discrepancy. The invoice describes the goods in words that do not quite match the credit.

This happens every day on the corridor, and the rulebook expects it. Under UCP 600 the issuing bank may ask its client to waive the discrepancy, and it has five banking days to pay or refuse. Asking does not stop the clock. So the work starts to move. Operations writes to the relationship manager. The relationship manager calls the client. Compliance screens the documents again, because a waiver leads to a payment and payments are screened. Credit confirms how the payment will be funded. Treasury prices the currency. If the wording problem is unusual, legal is asked for a view. Client service takes the client’s call asking where things stand.

Seven desks touch it, and each does its part correctly. By the time the waiver comes back, three of them have asked for something another desk already knew, two have waited a day for a reply, and the one thing that settled the outcome is in nobody’s system. Why is this discrepancy harmless for this client, when the same one would not be for another? The relationship manager knew, because she has watched this client trade for years. That knowledge went home with her at six.

Nothing went wrong. Every function performed. Nobody owned the work.

For forty years our software has respected that arrangement, for good reasons. A CRM holds what sales knows; an HR system holds what HR knows. Systems of record mostly stored information and coordinated people, and the org chart was a sensible boundary for both jobs. AI is different in kind. It performs the work. And work is a chain of actions, decisions and system calls that starts in one place and ends in another, crossing whatever functional lines it must on the way.

So an enterprise that buys AI for support, AI for sales, AI for finance and AI for HR is not changing how it operates. It is automating its fragmentation. Department heads will report better numbers, and the numbers will be real. The organisation will keep the same shape. The companies selling function-specific AI are right about the technology and wrong about the unit of analysis.

I hold that argument. It is also incomplete, in the place that decides whether it works. Enterprises have tried to organise around work before, more than once, and each attempt snapped back. This essay is about why they snapped back, what is different now, and what an enterprise looks like when the difference is taken seriously: how it scales, how its structure changes, and how its people learn.

A note on examples: the letter-of-credit case is built from the public rulebook and from how trade finance works across the industry, not from any institution I have worked with. The point is the shape of the work.

The argument in 60 seconds

Functions exist because context is expensive to move. Inside a function, people share vocabulary, systems and judgement, so context is dense and cheap. Between functions it has to be squeezed into a ticket or an email, and most of it is lost at the seam. Function-specific AI makes each function faster and leaves the seams where they were. Earlier attempts to organise around process failed for the same reason. They could redraw the boxes and could not move the context. Agents can carry context across a seam and record it as they go, which makes the seam the place where enterprise value is now made or lost. Two corrections keep this from becoming a vendor pitch. Some seams exist on purpose and must get harder. And the asset that compounds is the context layer every agent draws on, not any one agent. Structure follows. Functions become houses of depth, flows become the way work runs, and learning has to be redesigned, because agents now do the repetitions that used to train people.

01 · SignalA flat drawing of a three-dimensional thing

Every org chart has two axes. Across the page run functions, grouped by what people know how to do. Down the page run levels, grouped by how much people are trusted to decide. Every reorganisation I have watched moved boxes within that plane. It merged two columns, added a layer or removed one.

Work does not live in that plane. It moves through it, along an axis the chart has no way to draw, which is time. A request enters at one box, gathers facts and decisions, and leaves through another box days later. The chart tells you where people sit. It cannot tell you where the work goes, how long it waits, or what it forgets on the way. Motion 1, at the top of this page, is the same org chart turned ninety degrees.

Why are enterprises built this way? The usual answer is specialisation, from Adam Smith’s pin factory to Taylor’s stopwatch to Sloan’s divisions. That is true, and it leaves out the deeper reason, which Herbert Simon gave in 1962 with a parable about two watchmakers. Tempus assembles each watch from a thousand parts in a single sequence, so every interruption sends the half-built watch back to pieces. Hora builds from stable subassemblies of about ten parts, so an interruption costs only the piece in hand. Hora prospers and Tempus loses his shop. Complex systems that survive, Simon argued, are nearly decomposable. Interaction is dense inside each module and sparse between them.

Functions are Hora’s subassemblies. Inside a function people share a vocabulary, a set of systems, a professional standard and a great deal of unwritten judgement, so context is dense and cheap to use. Between functions interaction is kept sparse on purpose, and context has to be compressed into something that can cross: a form, a ticket, a status, an email. Jay Galbraith made the same point from another direction in 1974. An organisation is an information-processing system, and its structure is a set of choices about how much information it has to process and where. Functions reduce the load. They are a compression scheme.

In The Gap Is The Equation I agreed with Jack Dorsey and Roelof Botha that hierarchy is an information-routing protocol built for the bandwidth of people. This is the other half of that idea. Hierarchy routes context up and down. Functions store it side by side. Nothing in the classical design carries it across.

02Context dies at the seams

Context is a loose word, and the looseness is expensive, so here is how I use it. Data is what a system of record holds: the balance, the limit, the document. Context is what a person needs in order to act well on that data. It has five parts, and they survive a handoff very differently.

Each crossing loses some of it, and small losses compound. Fig. 2 is the arithmetic. If a handoff passes on 85 per cent of what the previous desk knew, seven handoffs deliver about a third and ten deliver a fifth. The rates are illustrative. I have no defensible measurement of how much a handoff loses, and neither does anyone else, because nobody records what was not passed on. What the model gets right is the shape. Loss compounds by multiplication, so the number of seams matters more than the quality of any single one.

Lost context does not disappear quietly. It comes back as three costs that everyone recognises and nobody books.

  • Re-asks. The next desk asks for what the last one knew, and the client is asked again for what they already said. In The Search Tax I described an agent’s retrievals as line items for things the organisation already knew and failed to hand over. A re-ask is the same line item, paid by a person, and often by the client.
  • Waiting. Work queues at every boundary. Michael George, who carried lean methods into service operations, found that most processes spend under a tenth of their elapsed time on work a customer would pay for. The rest is mostly waiting, and the waiting gathers at handoffs.
  • Escalation to whoever remembers. When context is missing, work goes to the person who holds it. In The Empty Window I argued that the knowledge that decides outcomes sits with a few dozen experienced operators rather than in documents. The seams are why those people are always busy.

This joins two earlier arguments. The Amnesia Tax is what an institution forgets across time, the judgement that goes home at close of business. The seam is where it forgets across space, the context that never reaches the next desk. It is the same tax collected in a different place, and in both places the costliest item is the judgement.

03 · MeaningWe have tried to organise around work before

None of this is new, and that is the strongest objection to it.

In 1990 Michael Hammer told readers of the Harvard Business Review to stop automating old work and obliterate it. Reengineering would organise companies around processes instead of departments. It produced real wins and a famous footnote. In the book that followed, Hammer and James Champy offered what they called an “unscientific estimate” that between half and seven in ten reengineering efforts fell short of the dramatic results intended. Hammer later complained that the estimate had been turned into a law. It was an observation, which is the right way to hold it. Business process management suites came next, then ERP with its best-practice processes, then robotic process automation. Before all of them, the matrix organisation tried to give work and function equal standing on paper.

Each of them modelled the process as its designers imagined it. Safety researchers have a precise name for the gap that opened, work-as-imagined against work-as-done. The happy path could be drawn as swim lanes and codified. The exceptions could not, because exceptions are settled in language, in calls and emails and notes in the margin, and they depend on exactly the context that lives in people. So every exception went back to a person in a function. Over time the exceptions became most of the work, the people who held the context became the process, and the organisation drifted back to the shape that kept that context close.

Reengineering could redraw the boxes. It could not move the context. Agents are the first technology that can.

Two things are different now.

The first is that agents work in language. For earlier automation, the exception was where the structure ran out and the machine stopped. Reading the email, weighing the note and interpreting the clause are what an agent does natively.

The second matters more. An agent that carries a piece of work across a seam is also an instrument. As it goes, it can record what it was given, what it asked, what it was told, what it decided and on whose authority. The trace of the flow becomes the seam record. This is the real reason the unit of analysis matters, and it is a stronger reason than efficiency. A function-specific agent never sees the seam, so it can never capture it. Only something that travels with the work can write down what the work knew.

Jaya Gupta and Ashu Garg of Foundation Capital have argued that the next systems of record will hold decisions rather than objects, and they call the accumulated traces a context graph. I agree, and I would add a location. The traces worth most are the ones at the seams, because that is where context is otherwise lost.

The survey data says the same thing from the other end. Of twenty-five organisational attributes McKinsey tested in early 2025, fundamental redesign of workflows had the largest effect on whether a company saw bottom-line impact from generative AI, and only about a fifth of companies had done it. In this year’s survey, nearly three-quarters of the six per cent McKinsey calls high performers report redesigning workflows, against about a quarter of everyone else. Even the headline adoption figure is organised by function. It counts companies that use AI in at least one business function.

04Your agent topology will become your org chart

In 1968 Melvin Conway observed that organisations design systems that copy their own communication structures. Software teams have since learned to run the observation in reverse. Draw the team boundaries you want and the architecture follows them.

Function-specific AI is Conway’s law running forward at machine speed, and it gets bought that way because budgets are functional. The head of support buys a support agent; the head of finance buys a finance agent. Each purchase is sensible and has a business case its owner can defend. Together they write today’s seams into software, with separate agents, separate context stores, separate integrations and separate definitions of who the customer is. Reorganisations come every few years. Integrations and data contracts outlast them. An enterprise that deploys one agent per function is setting its current org chart in concrete.

In The Levels of an AI-Pilled Organization I called this Level 2, where each function is more productive and each has its own private stack. The test for leaving it was a single question. Does the workflow cross a team boundary? Function-specific AI fails that test by design.

This changes who has to buy. A support tool is a departmental decision. A system that runs work across support, operations, compliance and finance is an operating-model decision, and its value appears in places no single function owns: end-to-end lead time, work done right the first time, the client who was not asked the same question three times, the capital not tied up because a trade settled a day sooner. Nobody owns the P&L of a seam, and so nobody buys for it. That makes it the chief executive’s problem by definition. McKinsey found the same at larger companies, where CEO oversight of AI governance was the attribute most associated with bottom-line impact.

Microsoft’s 2025 Work Trend Index proposed a structural answer. Replace the org chart with a Work Chart, in which teams form around outcomes and disband when the job is done, like a film crew, with “no reorg required”. I agree with the diagnosis and distrust the remedy. A film crew can disband because the film is finished and the film holds everything it needs. A client relationship is never finished. When the team that served a client this quarter disbands, what it learned goes with it unless something stays behind to hold it. Outcome teams without a shared context layer are a faster way to forget.

05Not every seam is an accident

Here I part company with the cleanest version of the argument, the one in which a single AI layer carries a process to completion regardless of how many departments it touches. In a regulated institution, regardless is the dangerous word.

Some seams exist because people could only hold so much. Call them coordination seams. They are accidents of bandwidth, and closing them is most of the prize.

Other seams exist on purpose. The maker who prepares a payment is kept apart from the checker who releases it. Credit risk stands independent of the business that originates the loan. Information barriers keep what the advisory side knows about a pending deal away from the people who trade. Customer data collected for one purpose may not be used for another. Call these control seams. They are the controls themselves, and an agent that carries all context across every boundary is a compliance incident with good latency.

So the requirement is sharper than crossing functions. Close the coordination seams. Make the control seams harder, by enforcing them where the agent runs: which context may cross, in which direction, to whom, under what authority and with what record. That is the control plane I argued for in When Intelligence Gets Arms and Legs, given a specific first job. The agent has to know which kind of seam it is crossing, and that knowledge cannot sit only in its instructions, where a clever prompt can talk it out. It has to be enforced in the path the work takes.

A seam audit therefore classifies before it optimises, and treating the two kinds alike is the error in either direction. Close a control seam and you have an incident. Defend a coordination seam as if it were a control and you keep the waiting, the re-asks and the overloaded experts, and call it prudence.

06Context compounds when it connects

Why should one enterprise-wide approach outperform a set of excellent functional tools? Because of how context behaves once it is shared.

In a function-specific deployment, context adds up. The support agent’s memory improves the support agent. The credit agent’s memory improves the credit agent. Ten functions, ten separate gains.

In a shared context layer, a record captured by one flow is available to every other flow that touches the same client, counterparty, shipment or contract. The number of useful connections grows roughly with the square of what is connected, the way the value of a network grows with the pairs it makes possible. The relationship manager’s note that this client routinely accepts a particular kind of wording discrepancy helps operations today, helps credit at the next limit review, and helps whoever drafts the client’s renewal. Skills compound the same way. Fix how one flow reads a document type and every flow that uses the skill is fixed.

Three limits apply, and each is a design requirement in disguise.

The effect flattens and can turn negative. Data network effects are real but weaker than pitch decks suggest. Most of the value arrives with the first connections, and stale context subtracts. In The Search Tax I called the failure confident staleness, where a layer that is certain and out of date makes agents stop early and act on last year’s answer. Shared context needs supersession and expiry, or its network effect runs backwards.

Connection needs identity. The client in the trade system, the client in the credit system and the client in the screening system have to be resolvably the same client. In most enterprises they are not, quite. Entity resolution is the unglamorous floor every network effect stands on.

A connection that may not be used is not a connection. In The Disappearing Middle I separated finding information, which models increasingly do for themselves, from governing it: who may see which document, which version is current, and proving afterwards what was consulted. Here the governing work decides which links in the network are allowed to exist. Governance sets the shape of the network as well as its limits.

07How the enterprise scales now

For most of the last century, enterprises scaled by adding people and paid for it in coordination. Fred Brooks put it plainly in 1975. Communication paths in a group grow with the square of its size, which is why adding people to a late project makes it later. Functions and hierarchy were the answer. They cap the paths by keeping most communication inside modules and routing the rest up and down a tree. They also cap the result. In The Gap Is The Equation I used Mathias Frey’s observation that effective capacity grows roughly with the logarithm of headcount. The seams are a large part of why that curve bends.

Agents change the cost of the expensive part. They raise what Galbraith called lateral capacity, the ability to coordinate across units without going up and down the hierarchy. His tools for it were liaison roles, integrators, task forces and, at the limit, the matrix, and every one of them consumed scarce human attention. An agent that carries context across a coordination seam is an integrator that does not tire and does not forget, provided the context is captured.

That proviso changes what scale is made of. Scale used to be bought with headcount. Increasingly it is bought with context, meaning the share of the enterprise’s work captured well enough for an agent to carry and connected well enough for the next flow to reuse. Two enterprises with the same models, the same platform and the same headcount will diverge on that number.

The same logic moves the edge of the firm. Ronald Coase asked in 1937 why firms exist at all if markets work, and answered that the cost of transacting decides what is done inside and what is bought. Agents lower the cost of coordinating inside the firm and, as counterparties deploy their own, the cost of transacting with the outside. Both pressures act at once. What stays distinctively inside is what the firm knows that nobody else does. The boundary of the firm drifts towards the boundary of its context.

Scale has a darker twin. A firm that can run a flow ten thousand times a day can be wrong ten thousand times a day. The blast radius grows with the reach, which is why the control seams of section 05 matter more as the flows get faster.

08What the structure becomes

Structure will not change in one move, and it will not end where the most excited forecasts say. Motion 3 shows the sequence I expect, in four stages. Most enterprises I see are somewhere between the second and the third.

Functions become houses of depth

They do not disappear. The case for depth I made in The Capability–Value Gap is a case for functions. An agent running a trade-finance flow has to command trade-finance practice precisely, and that precision is grown and owned by the people who practise it. What changes is the function’s job. It stops being where the work is done and becomes the owner of how the work is done well: the skills agents use, the policies they obey, the evaluations that define good, the exception desks that handle what the skills cannot, and the careers of the people who hold the craft. Ramp, which I wrote about in the levels essay, consolidated hundreds of specialised agents into one agent with thousands of skills. Read as structure, that is the pattern. One runtime carries the work, and the functions author the skills.

Flows get owners

Someone becomes accountable for an outcome a client would recognise, such as an account opened, a letter of credit paid or a claim settled, from end to end, with authority over how the flow runs and the measures it is judged on. In the function era that person did not exist, which is why the seam belonged to nobody.

Management changes its job

Much of middle management has always been context routing: knowing who knows, carrying the summary up, translating between functions. Agents take most of that. What remains grows. It is judgement on the hard cases, accountability for outcomes, and the coaching that turns juniors into experts. In a regulated institution a named person stays accountable for consequential actions. A world model can replace the routing. It does not replace the signature.

Decision rights get written down

Agents cannot act on implied authority. Every delegation has to be explicit about which decisions, up to what limit, whether reversible, and escalated to whom. In the levels essay I suggested defining authority by reversibility rather than by rank. Flow-based structure forces the question, because the hierarchy no longer routes the work past the person who would have stopped it.

New roles appear at the joints

Context stewards decide what is current and what has been superseded in a domain. Skill authors, usually senior practitioners, encode the craft. Evaluators maintain the test sets that define good. Flow engineers map work-as-done. Agent operations watch the runtime the way a control room watches a grid.

Measures move to the flow

Functional KPIs reward local optimisation, which is how every desk can hit its number while the client waits. Flow measures are end to end: lead time, right first time, handoffs per outcome, re-asks per case, exceptions escalated, and what the Amnesia Tax called judgement yield, the reusable improvement gained from each expert correction.

09Deployment is discovery

Cross-functional AI cannot be bought off a shelf and installed, and the reason is instructive. Process documentation describes work-as-imagined. The agent has to run work-as-done. Closing that gap takes engineers in the room where the work happens, in-house or embedded from a partner, building integrations, finding the edge cases and iterating until the agent completes the work end to end. MIT’s study of enterprise generative-AI pilots found that systems built with external partners reached production about twice as often as internal builds. Much of the reason, I suspect, is that good partners bring the discipline of living inside the work.

Two cautions follow.

Embedded engineers are context-capture devices. What they learn about how the institution actually runs, the undocumented exception, the informal approval, the reason a step exists, is the most valuable knowledge the programme produces. In the language of The Capability–Value Gap they are the discovery phase, and their output should be the specification, the evaluations and the seam records that let a flow be industrialised. If that knowledge lands in their notebooks instead of your context layer, you have paid the Amnesia Tax on your own transformation, and the partner leaves with the only accurate map of your institution. Make the capture a deliverable.

Starting in the hardest, highest-volume flow, often client service, is the right instinct. Be precise about what it proves. Running service end to end proves the plumbing: integrations, orchestration, identity and the control plane. Those carry over. It does not prove the context. What the service flow learned about service tells an agent nothing about credit. Success in one flow is evidence about the platform, and the knowledge still has to be earned flow by flow.

10Learning when the agents do the repetitions

Most transformation plans get this part wrong, and it is where the human cost is decided.

Experts are made by repetitions. Gary Klein’s studies of fire commanders and nurses, which I leaned on in The Search Tax, found that experienced practitioners search less than novices because they start from a prior built over thousands of cases. That prior comes from junior work: the hundredth document check, the fiftieth discrepancy, the routine case that teaches you what normal looks like so the abnormal one stands out.

Junior work is exactly what agents take first. Lisanne Bainbridge named the consequence in 1983, writing about industrial control rooms in a paper called “Ironies of Automation”. Automate the routine and you leave people with the rare, hard cases, while removing the practice that made them able to handle those cases. The early labour-market evidence fits. Using payroll records, Erik Brynjolfsson, Bharat Chandar and Ruyu Chen found a 13 per cent relative decline in employment for 22-to-25-year-olds in the occupations most exposed to AI, concentrated where AI automates rather than augments, while employment for experienced workers in the same occupations held up.

The ladder is losing its bottom rungs, and nobody has said where the next generation’s judgement will come from.

Four responses follow.

Manufacture the repetitions

If the work no longer produces practice, design the practice. The captured context layer is the raw material. Every exception an expert resolved, with its situation, reasons and outcome, is a case. An institution that captures its seams has, for the first time, a case library of its own work, and can train juniors on the cases its agents escalated last month. The same record trains the agents. One capture serves two learners.

Teach the new literacies

The scarce skills move. Specification means describing work precisely enough to automate, with acceptance criteria and named exceptions; in The Disappearing Middle I argued that most organisations are constrained by specification rather than by compute. Verification means knowing what a good output looks like and catching the confident wrong one. Flow literacy means understanding the whole flow, not one station on it. Exception judgement is the call the skill cannot make. Depth matters more than before, because depth is what writes the skills and catches the errors.

Redesign before you reskill

People cannot be trained into roles that do not exist yet. Redesign the flow, define the roles it needs, then train, and do the redesign with the people who do the work, because they hold work-as-done. Reskilling done to people produces resentment and side channels. Done with them, it produces the capture.

Capture the work, not the worker

Context capture can slide into surveillance, and when it feels like surveillance people route their real work around it, into personal chats, side calls and notes that never reach a system. The context layer then fills with the official story while the real context goes dark. Capture the decision and its reasons at the point of work, with purpose limits people can see, and leave the keystrokes alone.

The scale of this is large. Employers surveyed by the World Economic Forum expect 39 per cent of workers’ core skills to change by 2030. Of every hundred workers, they estimate fifty-nine will need training, and eleven of those are unlikely to get it. Those eleven are the cost of reskilling that waits for a course catalogue.

There is one more kind of learning, the organisation’s own. Chris Argyris distinguished single-loop learning, which corrects errors within the rules, from double-loop learning, which questions the rules. Functions do single-loop learning well, and each desk gets better at its step. Double-loop learning needs someone who can see the whole flow, and until now nobody could, because no record of the whole flow existed. The trace changes that. An organisation can only learn at the level at which it can see.

11Where I could be wrong

Functions are more than containers for context. They are professional identities, standards, communities and career ladders. People want a home, and matrix organisations have failed on human grounds as often as on technical ones. That is why I keep functions as houses of depth instead of dissolving them. If I have underestimated how much of a function’s value is belonging, flow structures will be pulled back into functions the way reengineering was, and the context layer will be the part that survives.

Accountability is assigned by function and by name. Regulators in most jurisdictions attach responsibility to named individuals and to independent control functions. In banks, structure will move at the speed of the accountability regime rather than the technology, and that may be years slower than this essay implies.

Some judgement will not be captured. Michael Polanyi’s observation that “we can know more than we can tell” has not been repealed. Capture will reach the reasons people state and much less of the judgement they cannot put into words. Where that judgement decides outcomes, the seam stays a human seam. The gain is then smaller, and the case for apprenticeship is stronger.

The one-system thesis may be right after all. If a single runtime with function-authored skills wins outright, as Ramp’s consolidation hints, my argument loses at the runtime and holds at the context layer. I can live with that. The asset I am arguing for is the layer, whichever agent reads it.

12 · ActionStart Monday: the seam audit

None of this needs a reorganisation to begin. It needs one flow, walked honestly.

  1. Pick one outcome a client would recognise. An account opened, a letter of credit paid, a claim settled. Use the client’s words, not a process name from the documentation.
  2. Walk it end to end with the people who do it. Count the functions it touches, the handoffs, the waits, and the re-asks, meaning every time someone asks for what someone else already knew.
  3. Classify every seam. Coordination or control. For each control seam, write down what may cross, what may not, and who says so.
  4. Define the seam record. For each coordination seam, name the five parts of context that must travel. Make the agent write the record as a by-product of doing the work, never as extra administration.
  5. Run the flow end to end. Function-authored skills, control seams enforced in the runtime, a named flow owner. Measure lead time, handoffs per outcome and re-asks per case, before and after.
  6. Read fifty seam records. Then decide what to build next. You will see your institution’s work written down whole for the first time.

Five questions for the next executive committee

  • Who owns each of our ten most important flows, end to end?
  • Which of our seams are controls, and where exactly are they enforced?
  • If we reorganised next year, which of our AI deployments would break?
  • What share of our work is captured well enough for an agent to carry it?
  • Where will our next generation of experts get their repetitions?

The org chart will survive. It will stop being the map of the work and become the map of where depth lives: who owns the craft, who is accountable, who trains whom. The map of the work will be the trace, and for the first time an enterprise will be able to read it.

Enterprises are organised into functions because context could not travel. Now it can, if you capture it, govern it, and decide which seams it must never cross. The firms that do will see their own work clearly for the first time. Most will be surprised by how much of it was waiting at a boundary.
