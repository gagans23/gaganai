The Philosophical Ledger · Agentic AI strategy

Rent the model. Own the harness.

In agentic AI, the question that decides who wins is not which model is smartest. It is who holds custody of what your agents learn.

Gagan Sachdeva · 18 October 2026 · ~20 min read · Agentic AI strategy · Context & knowledge

Most enterprise AI strategy still starts with the wrong question: which model should we buy?

It is an understandable question. Models are the visible part. They arrive with benchmarks and launch events, and they get better every quarter.

But an agent is not a model. An agent is a model running inside a harness, fed by context. The model is the part you can rent. The harness and the context are where your institution's judgement accumulates.

Rent the model. Own the harness. And own it in the open.

That conclusion rests on a chain of reasoning, and it is only as strong as its weakest link. Here is the chain. Each step is argued below, and then the whole thing is tested against the strongest case against it.

The argument in six steps
  1. A model is probabilistic. It samples an answer; it does not look one up. Reliability therefore has to be engineered around it.
  2. That engineering lives in the harness: the checks, tools, limits and routing that turn a likely answer into a dependable step.
  3. What the agent learns accumulates in the same place: memory, corrections, refined prompts, procedures.
  4. If the harness is tied to one model vendor, so is everything the agent has learned. Switching models then means forgetting.
  5. So the harness is the custody layer. For the vertical work that carries an institution's revenue and risk, it has to be owned.
  6. Open source is the cheapest way to own it, but ownership is a running cost. A harness nobody maintains decays without telling anyone.

01 · The stackWhat a harness actually is

An agent has three parts. The model reasons. The context is what the agent knows about your world.

The harness is everything else. It is the loop that calls the model, the tools the agent may use, and how prompts are shaped for each model. It decides which model handles which step, what gets remembered, what gets checked, and how much may be spent.

A banking analogy makes this concrete. The model is the trader: talented, fast, occasionally brilliant, and fallible in ways that are hard to predict. The harness is the desk around the trader. Firms hire and replace traders all the time. No serious firm outsources its desk, because the desk is where the institution's mandate, limits and memory live.

Two lessons follow. A great desk makes an average trader safe and a great trader productive; a great trader with no desk is a liability. And when a trader leaves, the desk carries on. The same should be true when you change models.

The context and the harness are where an institution's advantage compounds. The model improves on someone else's roadmap, which is fine, provided you can change suppliers without losing the desk.

02 · SteeringWhy LLMs need steering

Agents are hard to build because a probabilistic component sits at the centre of a system that has to behave reliably. Understanding why is the key to understanding why the harness matters.

How a model actually produces an answer

A large language model does not look answers up. It predicts. Given everything in its context, it assigns a probability to every possible next token (a word or part of a word), picks one, appends it, and repeats until it stops.

Picture a model that has read a letter of credit and is completing the sentence “The latest shipment date is…”. It puts most of its probability on the date in the credit itself, some on a date from an amendment it half-noticed, and a little on other candidates. Usually it picks the right one. Sometimes it does not.

A setting called temperature controls how often the model takes the less likely path. Turning it down helps, but it does not remove the tail: in this illustration the cautious setting still picks the wrong date about once in every eighteen runs. Even at the most conservative setting, small differences in how calls are served can change outputs.

A one-in-eighteen error rate is fine for drafting an email. It is not fine for a shipment date on a letter of credit. This is not a defect the next release will fix. Generation is sampling from a distribution, and every distribution has tails. The question is never whether the model will be wrong, but what catches it when it is.

Why this matters more for agents than for chat

A single answer that is right most of the time is useful; a person reads it and catches the slip. An agent is not a single answer. It chains many calls, each acting on the output of the last, often with nobody reading in between. Small per-step error rates multiply. Try it.

Back to the desk: this is a gifted trader working on instinct. Usually right, occasionally confidently wrong, and more error-prone the longer they go without a check. No bank lets that trader run a book for a day without limits and reconciliation. Nobody should let an agent run a twenty-step task without the equivalent.

How the harness steers

You cannot make a probabilistic model deterministic. You can surround it with deterministic code that narrows what it may do, checks what it did, and catches it when it drifts.

The model’s habitWhat goes wrong in practiceHow the harness steers
VarianceTwo runs give different verdicts on the same fileFixed output schemas; low temperature for decisions; critical judgements run more than once and compared; plain code for anything that can be calculated
SensitivityA reworded request breaks a working flowTested prompt templates per model, under version control, with regression evals on every change
Fluent confidenceAn invented clause, figure or citationAnswers grounded in retrieved sources with citations; tools called instead of facts recalled; verifiers that check claims against the source
No memoryA decision from last week is forgottenMemory, and context assembled fresh for every call
Uneven attentionThe key clause is missed in a long fileContext engineering: retrieve less but better, summarise, split work across sub-agents with clean contexts
Out-of-distributionDomain rules are misappliedDomain tools, skills and explicit checklists; narrow tasks routed to specialised models
Compounding errorLong tasks fail somewhere in the middleWork broken into checked steps, progress checkpointed, retries with feedback, escalation to a person
Any of these, actingA wrong payment instruction or email goes outTools scoped to the user's permissions, sandboxed execution, human approval for consequential actions

Watch what that does to thirty runs of the same task.

Put together, those features form a loop around every step the agent takes.

That is how a probabilistic component becomes part of a dependable system, and why reliability is engineered in the harness, not bought with the model. Over the last few years agents improved for two reasons: better models and better harnesses. Only the second one is yours.

Practitioners already know this. In industry developer surveys, accuracy has ranked as the top concern for building agents two years running, at roughly double the next two, latency and cost. In large enterprises cost is now climbing the list, and security ranks higher than elsewhere. Every one of those concerns is addressed mainly in the harness.

03 · CustodyThe custody problem

Here is the core of the argument. An agent in production accumulates state. It builds memory from conversations, learns facts about the environment, collects corrections from reviewers, and refines the prompts and tool schemas that make it work.

That state lives in the harness. The harness decides what is stored, in what format, how it is retrieved, and how it is presented to the model. So whoever controls the harness has custody of everything the agent has learned.

Now follow the dependency chain, as Motion 1 does at the top of this page. If your context is shaped around one harness, and that harness is built around one model provider, then your accumulated intelligence is tied to that provider. Switching models stops being a configuration change. It becomes a migration, and you pay for it by forgetting.

The real lock-in in agentic AI is not the API contract or the price per token. It is the cost of walking away from what your agents know.

Control of the harness gives you two things at once:

  • Offence. When a better model ships, from any lab, you can adopt it the same week. Model leadership has changed hands many times; your agents should be able to follow it.
  • Defence. A provider cannot reprice you, rate-limit you, deprecate a model under you, or change its usage policy without you having somewhere to go.

In a bank we would recognise this instantly. You do not leave client assets with a custodian you cannot exit. Context is an asset. Treat it like one.

04 · VerticalHorizontal agents rent, vertical agents own

Model labs train for what they see most, and what they see most is code. Their own harnesses are tuned for the same work. Inside that distribution, a lab's harness and model are co-designed and very hard to beat.

Step outside it and the picture changes. Take a trade-finance agent checking documents on a letter of credit moving along the UAE–India corridor. What counts as a discrepancy is defined by published rulebooks such as UCP 600 and ISBP, and by years of operator judgement about which deviations matter. None of that is in-distribution for a general model. All of it has to be written somewhere: into the tools, the checks, the sequence of steps and the verifier that signs off. That somewhere is the harness.

So the market splits cleanly:

  • Horizontal agents (general assistants, coding, enterprise-wide chat) can sensibly run on a lab's harness. The work is generic and the lab's tuning is an advantage.
  • Vertical agents (credit, trade, compliance, treasury, claims) win on domain judgement. They need a harness you can open, change and inspect.

The two will meet. The likely shape of the next internet is agent-to-agent: horizontal assistants calling the vertical agents that each provider exposes. A provider's own agent knows its customers and its systems better than any outside agent can. Every serious institution will need one, and every one of those is a harness it must own.

There is a practical reason this shift will happen whether institutions plan for it or not. Agents generate far more traffic than people do. One person asking an agent to watch for an available slot every few minutes produces hundreds of times the queries that person would make by hand. Providers are already blocking accounts for this. The sustainable answer is for each provider to expose its own vertical agent, which can reach its own systems efficiently, apply its own rules, and still recommend relevant products inside its natural-language replies. Building that agent well is a harness problem.

05 · RoutingRouting is a harness decision

Once you accept that no single model is best at everything, you need routing: sending each step to the model that does it well enough at the lowest cost. The question is where routing should live.

A gateway sees a request. A harness sees the task. That difference decides the quality of the routing.

What the router can seeGatewayHarness
The single request payloadYesYes
Which task and sub-agent produced the callNoYes
Whether the prompt cache is warm for the current modelNoYes
How each model wants to be promptedNoYes
Whether the last routing choice led to a good outcomeNoYes

In a vertical, routing decisions look like this. Sorting incoming documents by type is high-volume and simple, so it goes to a small, cheap model, possibly one post-trained for the job. Extracting fields goes to a mid-tier model. Judging whether a discrepancy is material goes to the frontier model, with a verifier behind it. A good desk works the same way: junior staff book the routine tickets, and the senior trader takes the hard calls.

Cache awareness alone changes the economics. Switching models mid-session throws away a warm cache, so sometimes the cheapest choice is to stay put. Only the harness can price that trade-off. One coding-agent company reported that harness-level routing cut aggregate cost by 58% against sending every call to the frontier model.

Gateways still matter. They are excellent at one API key for many providers, failover, and policy enforcement. The sensible split is that the harness decides which model, and the gateway decides how the call is executed and governed.

Notice the trap, though. If you run on a harness you cannot modify, the gateway is the only place left to route, and it routes blind. That alone is an argument for a harness you can change.

One honest caveat. The value of routing is not stable. When a new frontier model arrives that is simply better at everything, a carefully tuned routing table can lose much of its advantage overnight. That is not an argument against routing. It is an argument for keeping routing in code you control, so you can retune it the week a new model lands rather than waiting for someone else to.

06 · VerificationVerification is the moat, and it needs a writable harness

If an agent's value is proven by verification, value flows to whoever can write the verifiers. That means domain experts: people who can say precisely what a correct credit memo, a clean document set or a compliant disclosure looks like. Building those evals is slow, unglamorous work, which is exactly why it is defensible.

On a trading floor this is the middle office: the people who check every booked trade against what was actually agreed. Nobody serious treats the middle office as overhead. It is what lets the front office move fast without blowing up.

Public benchmarks are little help. Every new model is tuned against them, so they tell you less each release. Your own domain evals are the instrument to trust, provided you keep evolving them and keep reading actual traces rather than one summary number.

Verification is also what turns an agent into a learning system. The inner loop stays the same: a model calling tools. The outer loop is what makes the system better as it runs.

On a closed harness you can still edit what the agent remembers, but not how it works. In a vertical, most of the lessons that matter are about how it works.

07 · The ladderThe ladder of owning intelligence

There are four ways to make an agent yours, and each costs more than the last.

  1. Control the context. Your data, memory, glossaries and tools, on an off-the-shelf harness and model. Cheapest, and the place to start.
  2. Change the harness. Encode your workflow, checks, routing and guardrails in code. More effort, much more control.
  3. Post-train open-weight models. Worth it for narrow, high-volume sub-tasks such as classification, extraction, summarisation, guardrails and routing. Do it only once you have real users and real traces.
  4. Train from scratch. Almost nobody outside a frontier lab should.

The order matters. Even the teams publicly “building their own models” are mostly on rung three, and they got there only after squeezing everything out of context and harness.

Note what a closed harness does to this ladder: it removes rung two. It also makes rung three awkward, because today's open-weight models earn their keep on the narrow sub-tasks inside a harness, alongside a frontier model running the core loop. Mixing models like that needs a harness that is neutral about which models it calls.

Open weights matter for a second reason in regulated firms: where the data goes. Some institutions avoid open-weight models over fears that data will flow back to whoever built them. When you host the weights yourself, or with a provider under your contract and jurisdiction, that fear is largely unfounded: the model is a file, and nothing leaves your environment. The remaining concern, whether a model's training makes it behave in ways that conflict with your values or rules, is real in some domains, and it is exactly what your own evals exist to test. For institutions with data-residency obligations, self-hosted open weights inside an owned harness are often the cleanest way to keep both data and context at home.

08 · MemoryMemory is where the value compounds

If context is the asset, memory is how the asset grows. A model is the same on day 300 as on day one. An agent with good memory is not: it knows the exceptions, the house style, the counterparties that always send documents late, the reviewer who rejects vague rationale. That difference is the compounding return on every task the agent has already done.

Without memory, an enterprise pays for the same lesson again and again. Every session starts cold, every correction is lost, and people re-explain what the institution already knew. Memory turns usage into learning, and learning into an advantage a competitor cannot rent.

Four kinds of memory, four owners

“Memory” is not one thing. Enterprises that treat it as one database get one muddle.

The hard part is not storage

Storing memory is easy; some teams keep it in a single file. The hard part is distillation: deciding which of thousands of observations deserve to become durable knowledge, and in what form. That is a reflection task, and models are still weak at it.

Nobody has a general answer yet. What works is domain-specific: what is worth remembering for a music app is useless for a credit file. General-purpose memory products have struggled for exactly this reason. Expect to rebuild your memory design several times, which is only possible if you control it.

This changes what a “context layer” should be. Traditional databases are very good at storing and querying data the way they always have. An agent's context layer needs something more: an agent around the database, deciding what to write, what to merge, what to retire and what to surface. The database is the easy, buyable part. The judgement wrapped around it is the part each enterprise has to build, and it is the purest expression of the idea that the knowledge layer, not the model, is the moat.

What enterprises should work on

  • Define what is worth remembering, per domain. Write it down with the people who do the work. This is a business design task, not an IT one.
  • Give every memory a provenance. Where it came from, who confirmed it, when. A memory you cannot trace is a liability in a regulated firm.
  • Scope it to identity. Personal memory follows the user's permissions; institutional memory follows data classification. An agent should never recall what its user is not allowed to see.
  • Plan for forgetting. Memories go stale, contradict each other, or must be erased for retention and privacy rules. Expiry, correction and deletion are features, not afterthoughts.
  • Curate a core. A small set of human-approved institutional facts outperforms a large pile of unreviewed ones.
  • Measure memory quality. Evaluate whether recalled memories were relevant and correct, the same way you evaluate answers.
  • Keep it in formats you own. Plain, documented formats that any model can read. This is the custody problem again, at its most concrete.

09 · CostCost is a harness property

Token prices per unit of capability keep falling. Bills keep rising anyway, because agents and multi-agent systems consume far more tokens than chat ever did. Accuracy is still the first concern for most teams, but cost is climbing the list fast in large enterprises.

The controls that work are, in order:

  1. Visibility. Trace every agent run so you know who spent what, on which task, through which model.
  2. Budgets. Simple per-task and per-user limits, mainly as a guard against loops and misconfiguration. It takes only one badly configured tool that ships a full page snapshot back on every turn to burn five figures in a week.
  3. Harness engineering. Prompt caching, cache-aware routing, smaller models for narrow steps, and trimming what goes into context.

The first two can be bought. The third, which is where the large savings sit, needs a harness you can change.

10 · Open sourceWhy open source, and not merely neutral

Everything so far argues for a harness you control and that is neutral about models. A proprietary, model-agnostic platform could claim both. Open source adds five things a vendor cannot:

  • You can read what runs. In a regulated firm, the reviewer will ask how the system decides, not just which model it calls. Source you can inspect is the shortest path to auditability as provability.
  • You can fork it. If the maintainer changes direction, raises prices or disappears, your agents keep running.
  • You can delete code. As models absorb today's workarounds, good harnesses shrink. With open source you remove a workaround the week it becomes unnecessary, instead of waiting for a vendor's release.
  • You inherit open standards. Tool access through MCP, agent-to-agent calls through A2A, instructions in AGENTS.md, packaged expertise as Agent Skills. These keep your context portable beyond any one harness, too.
  • You get the ecosystem's speed. Harness patterns change every few months. An open project absorbs them from thousands of contributors faster than any single vendor.

What to check before you commit

Open source is necessary, not sufficient. A harness worth building on should pass this list.

11 · MaintenanceWhy the harness needs a maintenance budget

Owning a harness is not a one-off build. It is a living control system, and it decays even when nobody touches it, because everything around it keeps moving.

The desk analogy holds here too. No bank builds a risk function once and walks away. Risk models are recalibrated when markets move, limits are reviewed, procedures are rewritten after incidents, and supervisory guidance on model risk expects ongoing monitoring and revalidation on material change. A desk running last year's controls in this year's market is a desk waiting for a loss. A harness is the same.

What makes a harness drift

  • The model changes. A new version follows instructions differently, formats output differently, calls tools more or less eagerly. Prompts tuned for the old model degrade quietly.
  • Workarounds expire. Much harness code exists to cover a model weakness. Early agents parsed tool calls out of free text; once models gained native tool calling, that code became dead weight. Scaffolding that outlives its reason makes the agent worse, not safer.
  • Tools and systems change. APIs get new schemas, endpoints move, permissions are restructured.
  • The business changes. New products, policies and rules arrive, and yesterday's eval set stops describing today's work.
  • Prices change. New models, new caching features and new price points change what the cheapest good route is.
  • Threats change. New prompt-injection techniques, dependency vulnerabilities and sandbox weaknesses appear constantly.
  • Regulation changes. Obligations on explainability, retention and accountability tighten.

The danger is silent failure

Traditional software tends to fail loudly: it crashes, it throws an error, someone gets paged. A degrading agent does not crash. It gets slightly worse, a little more often, and stays just as fluent and confident while doing it. Nobody notices until a customer, an auditor or a loss does.

That is why evals and traces are maintenance tools, not just build tools. They are the only instruments that see quiet decay.

What good maintenance looks like

WhenWhat the team does
Every changeRun regression evals before any prompt, tool, memory or model change ships
Every weekDomain experts review a sample of traces; each lesson goes to context, harness or weights
Every monthReview cost and routing; prune and correct memory; add new failure cases to the eval set
Every model releaseShadow-test it on your own evals; then adopt it, route some tasks to it, or ignore it
Every quarterModel-swap drill in staging; dependency and security patching; delete scaffolding the models no longer need
Every rule changeUpdate the tools, policies and verifiers it touches

How to fund and own it

  • Fund it as a product, not a project. A harness needs a standing team and a run budget, the way a trading desk needs a standing risk function.
  • Name the owners. A platform team owns the shared harness. A domain owner owns each agent's evals and memory. Someone is accountable for every agent in production.
  • Make deletion a goal. A well-maintained harness gets thinner over time, as models absorb what used to need code. Measure that.
  • Let open source carry the commons. With an open harness, the community ships fixes for the shared layers. Your team maintains only what is genuinely yours: the domain logic, the verifiers, the memory design.

Maintenance is where the decision to own the harness is really paid for. It is also where the return compounds, because every lesson captured makes the next model swap, the next workflow and the next audit cheaper.

12 · The case againstThe strongest case against

This argument has real opponents, and they are not wrong about everything.

  • Co-design wins in distribution. A lab trains its model and its harness together. For coding and general assistance, that pairing is usually better than anything you will assemble. My answer: use it there. The argument here is about vertical work.
  • You own the maintenance. Harness code ages fast as models change. An open harness means your team patches, upgrades and secures it. My answer: yes, so budget for it as described above, keep the harness thin, and treat deleting code as a discipline.
  • Open does not mean portable. Prompts and tool schemas tuned for one model still need work to move. Portability is engineered, not licensed. My answer: test it. Swap the model in staging every quarter and measure what breaks.
  • Supply-chain risk. Open dependencies need vetting like any other code. My answer: pin versions, review changes, and prefer projects with clear governance.

None of these overturns the conclusion. They sharpen it: rent the lab's harness for horizontal work, own an open one for the vertical work that carries your revenue and your risk.

13 · ActionWhere enterprises should spend their energy

The scarce resource is not models or money. It is the attention of the people who know how the work is really done. Spend it on the layers no one can sell you.

Invest here
  1. Context capture. Glossaries, definitions, decision precedents, the exceptions operators carry in their heads. This is the raw material of every agent you will ever build.
  2. Memory design, per domain. What to remember, from whom, for how long, under which permissions. Start with one domain and get it right.
  3. Verifiers and evals. Pair domain experts with engineers to define what correct looks like, task by task. This is the moat.
  4. Tracing and the learning loop. Capture every run, review traces weekly, and route each lesson to context, harness or weights.
  5. An owned, open harness for vertical work. Thin, model-neutral, instrumented, with routing and caching in code, and a standing team and budget to maintain it.
  6. Identity and control. Agents act with user-scoped permissions, approvals on consequential actions, and an audit trail you can show a regulator.
Spend less energy here
  • Model selection debates. Pick a good default, keep the option to switch, and move on.
  • Training models from scratch. Climb the ladder in order; post-train narrow sub-tasks only once you have users and traces.
  • Building your own gateway, sandbox or vector database. These layers are maturing fast. Buy or adopt them.
  • Chasing public benchmarks. They measure the labs, not your work.

A simple test for any AI budget line: does it increase what the institution knows and can prove, in a form it keeps if the vendor changes? If yes, invest. If no, rent.

CloseRent the first. Own the second.

Models are becoming a utility. You should be able to switch them the way you switch a power supplier: on price, on performance, without rewiring the building.

The harness and the context are different. They are where an institution's judgement is written down, where its agents remember, and where it proves to itself and its regulators that the work was done right. The value of a deployed AI system converges to the quality of the knowledge layer beneath it, not the model within it; whoever holds that layer holds the value.

Rent the model. Own the harness. And own it in the open, because the one thing you cannot afford to rent is what your institution has learned.
