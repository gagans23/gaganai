Prologue

A Lesson from Gaudí

1 min read

Barcelona was generous to us. Blue skies, a soft November sun, and a city that makes you want to walk between sessions instead of looking at your phone.

It is hard to be here in 2026 and not think about Gaudí. This year marks a hundred years since his death, and the Sagrada Família, begun in 1882, is still being built. Gaudí did not draw his arches. He hung chains and weighted strings upside down and let gravity find the shape. He designed the constraints, and the system worked out the form.

I kept coming back to that image during the week, because it is a fair description of what an enterprise full of AI agents has to become.

His story has a second lesson. When his workshop burned in 1936, his plaster models were smashed, and builders spent decades piecing his intent back together from fragments. Computers eventually helped decode geometry he had carried in his head. His knowledge outlived him only because some of it had been written down.

Design the constraints. Capture the knowledge. Those two ideas turned out to be what the whole symposium was about.

My thanks to my firm’s leadership for the time and the trust to be here, and to Gartner for four days of this quality.

Prologue

The Paradox

4 min read · 1 figure

I went to Barcelona expecting four days about models. I came back with four days about everything around them.

The opening keynote gave me the sentence I carried through the week: “We are in a period of AI inflation during a trust recession.”

The numbers behind it were plain. In 2025, one AI project in five showed a positive return. In 2026, it is still one in five. Over the same two years, AI spending rose eighteenfold and the number of use cases more than tripled. Vendors promised productivity gains of 50% or more. The median gain organisations actually got is 16%. And 86% of CIOs now say AI risk is growing faster than AI value.

At the same time, intelligence has never been cheaper or easier to get. One session counted more than 40 frontier models released in a few months. The newest small models cost about a tenth of what their predecessors did. More than half of new code is now written by AI, and on one of the largest code platforms, agents open more than 40% of all pull requests.

Put those two sets of numbers side by side and you get the paradox that ran through almost every room. Intelligence has become abundant. Almost everything it depends on has stayed scarce.

Value is scarce. Control is scarce. Context, the knowledge that makes intelligence useful inside one particular business, is scarce. Security is scarce, because attackers got the same intelligence on the same day. People with the right skills are scarce. Trust is scarce. Even electricity has become scarce.

One speaker put it in five words that I heard, in different forms, in at least eight rooms: “Intelligence is abundant. Control is scarce.”

This essay follows that thought through. If intelligence is turning into a commodity, advantage moves to the scarce things around it. The seven parts that follow take them one at a time.

How to read this essay

I have written it the way I took it in: as a listener in the room, trying to connect what one session said with what another left out. It is long on purpose, and built so that you do not get lost.

There are seven parts, one for each scarce thing: money, control, knowledge, resilience, security, people and trust. Each part opens with a short page on why it follows from the one before. Each chapter opens with the question it answers and closes with the decision it asks of you. Where a Gartner framework helps, I have redrawn it as a figure, so you can see how the analysts think and not only what they concluded. Figures are numbered by chapter. Three chapters are case studies: agentic banking, the hollowing out of CRM, and quantum.

If you have ten minutes, read the one-page summary below and the questions at the end. If you have an evening, read it in order. Each part leans on the one before.

The argument on one page

  • Money.AI spending is running ahead of AI revenue, and a correction is coming. The real cost of AI sits in running it and in changing how people work, not in building it. Value only shows up when you divide AI cost by a business measure.
  • Control.Autonomy is a dial, not a switch. An agent earns it with evidence and an agreed error budget, and loses it the moment conditions change.
  • Knowledge.Models are rented and increasingly interchangeable. The context beneath them is what you own. Applications, CRM included, are becoming components on top of it.
  • Resilience.Sovereignty is optionality paid for in advance. Decide your minimum viable organisation before the crisis, not during it.
  • Security.Attackers have AI too, but defenders still hold the advantage if they move. Shadow AI now acts rather than leaks. Answer the board’s real question, how much security is enough, and start the post-quantum migration now.
  • People.The operating model is being rebuilt around people and agents together. Everyone is becoming a builder. The managers boards plan to remove are the ones who make the change stick. Decide which skills to protect, and rebuild the apprenticeship.
  • Trust.Integrity can be measured, customers are starting to arrive as software, and the ability to prove what is true is becoming part of the product.
  • What comes next.Gartner’s predictions and its 2027 technology trends all point at control, context and the ability to change, not at bigger models.

The essay draws on more than 90 sessions, grouped by theme rather than by day. I have left company and vendor names out on purpose. The ideas travel without them.

It also builds on things I have written before, and links to them where they apply: The Capability–Value Gap, The Empty Window, The Search Tax and The Layers You Cannot Buy.

Part I·Money—Chapter 1

The Bill Arrives: Who Pays When Spending Outruns Revenue

Chapter 1 of 27 · 5 min read · 1 figure

The question this chapter answers

Who pays for AI, and what happens when the spending outruns the revenue it was meant to create?

Picture a country building toll roads at record speed, years before the traffic arrives. The roads are good. The engineering is real. But if the cars come more slowly than planned, someone eventually has to admit the roads are worth less than they cost.

That is roughly where the AI industry stands. The “State of the AI Union” session started with that arithmetic rather than with the models, and I think it was right to.

Semiconductor revenue is on a new growth curve: about $1.7 trillion in 2026, $2.3 trillion in 2027 and $2.8 trillion by 2030. The largest cloud providers alone are expected to have spent around $6.3 trillion on AI infrastructure by 2030. Revenue from AI software, models, agents, platforms and data will not catch up in time. By 2030, AI revenue is forecast to fall more than $500 billion short of justifying what is being spent on AI-optimised servers, and over $4 trillion of capital spending will be exposed to write-downs if returns do not reach a normal cost of capital.

A write-down is the toll road moment: someone admits the asset is worth less than it cost. It does not stay on one balance sheet. It travels through suppliers, prices and markets.

The analysts drew the AI economy as a tower of blocks: investor confidence, future revenue expectations, cloud providers’ capital spending and infrastructure debt, stacked on top of each other. A safety incident, a geopolitical shock, a financial shock or an energy shock could each pull a block out. Their conclusion was the most quoted line of the week.

A correction will not land on everyone equally. Organisations in a weak financial position should play defence: keep cash, cut the AI portfolio back to what matters, reduce exposure and debt, and fund only the highest-value work. Organisations in a strong position should play offence: invest through the correction, pick up talent and capabilities while they are cheaper, and move faster towards an AI-native way of operating. The plan that does not work is having neither.

One consequence got less attention than it deserved. The cloud sessions predicted that by 2028 at least one of the eight largest cloud providers will fail to make its AI investment pay and will need a financial rescue, and that providers will raise prices on traditional services to recover what they spent on AI. Put those together and your AI supplier’s balance sheet belongs in your risk register.

Inside the enterprise, saved time is not saved money

The most rigorous session of the week analysed 2026 earnings calls from 1,200 large listed companies. Only 4% reported material, enterprise-wide cost reduction from AI. Among CFOs, 74% say AI saves time and 5% say it saves money.

That gap is the whole problem in one pair of numbers. Saved time does not leave the profit and loss statement. It turns into slightly less busy people, unless someone changes a contract, a structure or a constraint.

The companies that did cut cost asked a different question: what are we spending because it is necessary today, and could AI change the reason it is necessary? In financial services, the examples were specific. Fraud and credit losses that better prevention could avoid. Outsourced KYC and AML review that could come back in-house. Cash and liquidity buffers that better prediction could shrink. Reconciliation teams whose work could be organised differently. Each candidate then faces three tests. Constraint: what material cost is held up by something AI can change? Conversion: what must management actually do for that cost to fall? Claim: what result can finance credibly book?

Value is slowly moving from claimed to counted. In the earnings calls of more than 1,000 large non-tech companies, mentions of AI value that were specified or quantified, rather than just asserted, grew about 2.5 times in a year. When companies do count, value turns up in unglamorous places: fewer steps, fewer handoffs, fewer escalations, less rework.

The C-suite survey gave the sober baseline. 58% of functional leaders report a positive return from AI, 16% break even, 3% lose money and 23% do not yet know. For the use cases that work, the average return is about 15%. That is a good number. It is nowhere near what one analyst called the “hype-pothetical math” many boards now expect: a dollar back for every dollar spent, and fewer people.

Price is one number. Value depends on what you divide by.

The FinOps session explained the measurement problem with an old puzzle from Adam Smith. Nothing is more useful than water, yet it buys almost nothing. A diamond has almost no use, yet it buys a great deal. Municipal water costs $1 to $3 a cubic metre. The same water can fill a fountain or keep someone alive.

Price is one number, the same for everyone. Value depends on what was delivered, who needed it and when.

That is why metering was never the hard part. Any meter can read price. No meter can tell you value unless you give it a business measure to divide by.

The bridge between the two is a unit cost: AI and cloud spend over a period, divided by a business measure over the same period. Cost per transaction on a core banking system. Cost per customer record handled. Cost per citizen served. When unit cost falls while volume rises, value is being created. When spend jumps and the business measure does not move, an alert should fire before the month-end bill does.

The same session made a point about ownership that applies well beyond cloud bills: “Distributed rights and centralised accountability does not work. Ever.” If everyone can spend and only IT is accountable, nobody is in charge. The aim is for the people who create the spend to see it, forecast it and answer for it. One line stayed with me: human creativity is practically limitless, but your token budget is not.

The decisionEntry 01 · Ch. 1

Decide whether you are playing defence or offence in the coming correction, and make every AI line in the budget answer to a business measure: cost per transaction, per case, per customer served.

⁂

Knowing that the bill is coming is one thing. Knowing what is actually on it is another, and most budgets are missing the biggest lines.

What AI Really Costs: The Lines Missing From the Budget

Chapter 2 of 27 · 7 min read · 3 figures

The question this chapter answers

What will an AI programme actually cost once it is running, and where are the costs nobody planned for?

The session on what an AI programme really costs opened with a number that should worry every finance team. In 2027, 67% of companies will change their financial plans on the assumption that AI will have an impact. His question was simple: what will it cost to deliver on that promise?

The answer came in ten parts. I have grouped them into the four things I think a leadership team needs to understand.

Size: scale changes the bill by orders of magnitude

The median annual AI spend of a single business function is about €350,000 in a midsize enterprise, €2.4 million in a large one and €21 million in a global one. Gartner’s estimate for a baseline AI implementation in 2027, across IT and business budgets, is $390,000, $2.7 million and $23.5 million respectively. The first question for any board is whether its budget matches what its competitors spend, and what its own executives expect to get back.

Shape: you have three AI budgets, and they behave differently

This was the most useful idea in the session. Gartner separates AI value into three kinds of business case, and each one produces a different kind of budget.

Read the table by row. A Defend budget grows with headcount, like software licences always have. An Extend budget grows with business volume, which means it rises when the business does well, the opposite of what finance is used to. An Upend budget is funded in tranches, like a venture, and each tranche has to earn the next. Mixing all three into one “AI budget” is how organisations lose track of where the risk is.

Behaviour: AI costs move in ways IT has never planned for

Three findings changed how I think about AI cost.

The first is variance. 58% of CFOs rank software and licences as the fastest-growing AI cost, and estimates can go wrong by 500% to 1,000%. The cause is usually not the price of a token. It is usage: a solution planned for a few hundred users is rolled out to tens of thousands, each of whom uses it more than expected.

The second is concentration. Gartner showed the typical spread of individual AI spend. About 90% of users spend around €200 a month. About 10%, the power users, spend around ten times that. And the top 1% can run into tens of thousands a month. Token spend clusters in software development and marketing, and unbudgeted spend clusters in the top 1% to 5% of developers.

The fix is what the session called a “virtually unlimited” hard cap, set at about ten times a normal user’s spend. Normal users never hit it. Power users hit it occasionally. Nobody runs up a five-figure bill without someone looking at it first.

The third finding is the one that flips the usual business case:

In traditional IT, running a solution costs about 20% of what it cost to build. With AI, running it often costs as much as building it, or more. And the cost of changing how people work, which for an ERP programme runs at 15% to 20% of the implementation cost, runs at 100% to 200% for AI. For most organisations, operating model change will be the largest single cost of AI. It rarely appears in anyone’s budget.

Ownership: much of the spend is not in IT’s budget at all

Business leaders often spend more on AI than the CIO and CTO combined. AI embedded in existing business applications arrives as a price increase on software you already own, and for many companies that will be the largest AI cost of all. Talent, adoption and workflow redesign belong in the budgets of the business leaders who will benefit. If they are not there, the programme will stall for reasons nobody can see on a spreadsheet.

The session ended with two points I had not heard put this way before. Agents and people respond differently when the load increases. A team of people slows down and asks for more staff. A fleet of agents speeds up and sends a bigger bill. So the strategic question is not whether an agent is cheaper than a person. It is that the drivers of cost change, and with them, possibly, how you should price your own products. And organisations are already spending productivity gains they have not yet measured. If a 2027 financial plan assumes AI productivity, someone should trace every decision that assumption justifies, and plan for what happens if it arrives late.

Fund and govern each kind of spend differently

A second cost session, on moving from chaos to control, explained why AI bills are harder to manage than cloud bills. A cloud bill answers the question “what infrastructure did I run?”. It is driven by capacity and usage, and it is fairly predictable. An AI bill answers a different question: what did people ask, which model answered, and how? It is driven by behaviour and design, and it moves in ways that are hard to forecast. The list of things that move it is long: the size of prompts and answers, retries and verification steps, which model a request is routed to, how much autonomy an agent has, how many requests arrive at once.

AI cost also arrives in waves. First individuals experiment, with small, self-funded spend that shows up in expense claims. Then teams adopt, requests come in from across departments and spend grows fast. Then the business depends on it, and sourcing, monitoring and risk have to be run centrally. Each wave is announced by signals, and the session’s advice was to watch for them rather than try to stop the wave.

Its best line came with an analogy. Congratulations on your new bakery. Your first question should not be how much flour to order. Start with the value and the outcome, then work back to the cost. It then split AI spend into four segments by who benefits, each funded and governed in its own way:

Personal productivity spend benefits individuals. Its value is real but hard to track, so cap it, limit it per user, use cheaper standard models and prefer fixed-price deals. Functional spend benefits teams. Fund the functions that show measurable gains, tie extra spend to clear measures and keep alternative models in play. Business-critical spend benefits business units, so budget it against the outcomes it supports and allocate it by workflow, with specialised models, enterprise agreements and internal hosting where possible. Strategic investment benefits the whole organisation and behaves like a venture: it needs go and no-go criteria, leading indicators and flexible funding. The mix on the slide, 60%, 30%, 6% and 4%, was illustrative. The question beneath it was the right one: will your mix produce the value the business expects?

Two points belong next to the three budgets above. There is no single cost for an AI use case: the same business outcome can cost radically different amounts depending on how the solution is designed. And governance is worth most before costs scale, not after they appear on an invoice. The session’s summary: don’t govern all AI investments the same way; match governance to the value you want to create.

Keeping the bill under control

The tokenomics session offered five levers, in order. Set a budget ceiling. Make the efficient choice the default, such as a smaller model. Add friction to escalating to a bigger, costlier model. Show spend to whoever creates it. And reward value per token rather than tokens saved.

It also named three ways AI bills run out of control. A recursive multiplier: one task that retries endlessly can cost a hundred times what was expected. Context window inflation: a model that rereads the whole conversation and every attached document on each turn. And sending simple jobs to the most powerful and most expensive model.

The predictions session took this further. By 2028, it expects 60% of the world’s largest companies to build cost control into the inference path itself, with AI gateways that govern spend in real time instead of a monthly report that tells you too late. By 2029, 60% of organisations deploying AI will have a dedicated function to map AI’s total cost to value or profit. The advice I found most practical: instrument the inference path now, before agentic workloads make spend impossible to budget, and do not wait for a perfect value metric.

The decisionEntry 02 · Ch. 2

Split your AI spend into Defend, Extend and Upend budgets, put a “virtually unlimited” cap on individual spend, and make sure the cost of changing how people work sits in a business budget, not nowhere.

⁂

If the bill is this large and this unpredictable, the question becomes why so few organisations get value for it, and what the ones that do have in common.

The Maturity Chasm: Why One in Five Still Wins

Chapter 3 of 27 · 6 min read · 2 figures

The question this chapter answers

If almost everyone is spending, what separates the minority who get a return?

Where the market sits

Gartner placed generative AI in the trough of disillusionment on this year’s AI hype cycle, with agentic AI heading down towards it. The trough is the stage where the easy promises run out and the real work starts. Climbing out on the other side are the unglamorous enablers: AI governance technologies, FinOps for AI, AI agent identity, AI-ready data and knowledge graphs.

The first hype cycle dedicated to agentic AI added a reality check. Among less mature organisations, only 8% run a true agent, while 31% run something called an agent that lacks the capabilities of one. Among the most mature, 44% run true agents. Gartner calls the gap agent washing.

More use cases did not mean more value

The session on work redesign had a slide I photographed. Organisations have increased their number of AI use cases by 400%. Only 20% deliver measurable return. Only 2% reach disruptive, enterprise-level value.

The speaker’s diagnosis was that most organisations are layering AI onto yesterday’s processes: an AI step at intake, another at triage, another at review, while the process itself stays the same. Each function adds its own use cases, and the value line over time stays flat. The instruction was short: stop counting AI use cases, and start reimagining work.

He offered a way to choose where to start. Score each business capability on two things: the value AI could add, and how ready it is for AI. High value and high readiness: act now. High value and low readiness: build the advantage. Low value: accept a marginal gain. And he added a warning: today’s AI-ready capabilities are becoming tomorrow’s industry baseline. Being early on them is an advantage only for a while.

The most honest part of the session was about what happens next.

When you really redesign work, performance gets worse before it gets better. Costs rise and business measures slip while processes are redesigned, people are retrained, roles and decision rights change, and new technology and data are built. The return comes later, on the far side of the dip. Business leaders have to commit to staying through it. Most AI programmes are cancelled at the bottom of the curve, which is exactly where the investment has been made and none of the return has arrived.

What the winners do differently

Maturity, more than money, separates the winners. Only 17% of organisations are AI-mature, and they get 5.6 times as many AI initiatives fully deployed. They spend more, about 1.4% of revenue on AI against 0.2%, but above all they spend differently. They invest in durable assets, such as metadata and semantic layers, evaluation frameworks, guardrails and enterprise architecture, rather than in fast-changing parts like particular models and agent frameworks.

Every use case leaves something reusable behind: an evaluation suite, a design pattern, a value scorecard, a cost tracker. The session called this the AI staircase, or “build the builder”. 95% of mature organisations have a dedicated AI leader, against 56% of the rest, and AI leaders who work closely with the CEO are 3.4 times more effective. The largest CIO survey of the year found that only 11% of CIOs deliver both strong business outcomes and high AI value in the eyes of their CEO. Budget, company size and industry do not predict who they are. Behaviour does.

The investing rule came up in three different rooms: invest narrowly, and do not let a thousand flowers bloom. The best use cases move several outcomes at once, a lower price, a faster response and a lower cost together. High-return organisations spend about twice as much on foundations as their peers. As one speaker put it: no one-offs.

Two disciplines are worth adopting outright. The first is a stop rule, written before launch: “Expect [outcome] to move from [baseline] to [goal]. While [threshold] holds, [owner] reviews [source] and decides to scale, reset or stop.” The second is knowing when a big bet is worth making anyway. One chemicals company spent about $90 million on a custom model and follow-on research. The model found 180 new material compounds worth about $1.5 million each, roughly $270 million of value. The session’s advice: buy the position to upend, not the immediate return.

Modernise the core, one lap at a time

The agentic core session explained why so many programmes disappoint their CEO. Asked how their CEO rates the benefits the enterprise has had from AI so far, 67% of CIOs said none to some. Only 16% said considerable to exceptional. Speed to benefit is the largest gap between what CEOs expect and what IT delivers.

Gartner’s name for the foundation agents need is the agentic core: the business technology base that lets agents carry out, coordinate and improve work across enterprise processes. The session’s image was a racing car. You cannot run a high-performance engine in an outdated chassis. Monolithic systems, data locked inside core applications, workflows never designed for AI, and money still tied up in retiring old systems all hold agents back, however good the models are.

Its answer was to run modernisation as a race in laps, with pit stops in between. Each lap delivers a business capability people can see. Each pit stop builds a foundation they cannot see, which the next lap needs. The worked example was an insurer’s first notice of loss, the moment a customer reports a claim:

First, AI answers the calls. Then speech becomes text. Then the agent authenticates the caller and checks for fraud, finds the context of the claim, works towards resolving it, and finally hands off through a screen built for the moment. Underneath, the pit stops build what each lap needs: a modern contact centre, an AI platform, identity and access management, API access and tool discovery for agents, a decision and orchestration layer, and work redesigned around the result. The business sees cost per claim, handling time, customer satisfaction, fraud prevented and revenue retained improve lap by lap, long before the whole journey is finished.

The session summed up what winning CIOs do differently in five shifts. Fund capabilities, not use cases. Report value, not projects. Measure confidence, not outputs. Modernise the core before building agents. And compound small improvements rather than chase one big win. The point I took away: a CIO has to manage the CEO’s confidence as carefully as the technology, because the gap between expectation and delivery is where programmes get cancelled.

This is the pattern I described in The Capability–Value Gap: capability compounds, value stays flat, and whoever cannot say what their spend is for pays for the gap. What the symposium added is the part that should worry boards most. The market is about to put a price on that gap.

The decisionEntry 03 · Ch. 3

Write a stop rule before every launch, fund durable foundations ahead of fashionable tools, modernise the core in visible laps, and pick a few capabilities to redesign properly, with business leaders who will stay through the dip.

⁂

Every one of those redesigns hands work to software that acts. Which raises the next question: how much is each agent allowed to do on its own?

A Dial, Not a Switch: Reliability, Error Budgets and Earned Autonomy

Chapter 4 of 27 · 7 min read · 2 figures

The question this chapter answers

How much should an agent be allowed to do on its own, and who decides?

One of the reliability sessions opened with a question: imagine a watch that is right 90% of the time. Would you wear it?

Nobody would. Yet that is roughly the deal many organisations offer their customers when they put an impressive agent demo into production. Gartner predicts that 70% of agentic AI initiatives will fail by 2029, and the reason is not weak models. It is the distance between a demo that works and a system you can trust every day.

The opening keynote framed this better than anyone. You cannot run AI value and AI risk as two programmes, because they are one flywheel. Value creates exposure. Exposure, managed well, gives you the confidence to take on more value.

Most organisations have the flywheel running backwards. 71% will have deployed agentic AI; only 21% have governance for it. Only a quarter of CIOs say technology risk is built into major business decisions from the start. And 40% of workers received AI-generated “work slop” from a colleague in the past month, at a cleanup cost estimated at about $52 million a year for an average large listed company.

Then the keynote told the room to use less AI. The biggest drag on AI momentum, it said, is using AI where it is not needed. The rule it quoted from a manufacturer: eliminate, simplify, standardise, then automate. Use an agent when you need autonomy, not just automation.

The difference is easy to see. A password reset is a script: the same steps every time. A customer who is locked out, double-charged, stuck with a broken app and boarding a flight in an hour needs a plan made on the spot. That is a job for an agent. The small-team session drew the same line. Where work is predictable, ordinary automation is enough. Where it is completely open-ended, today’s agents are not ready. Agents earn their keep in between, where tasks change too much for fixed rules but repeat often enough to learn the pattern.

Reliability, not intelligence, is the bottleneck

Language models fail in surprising ways because their ability is uneven: two tasks of the same difficulty can land on opposite sides of what the model can do. Gartner calls this the jagged frontier. So reliability has to be engineered for the whole system, the way an aircraft is made safe rather than just its engine.

The sessions broke reliability into five properties. Accuracy: how often it works. Consistency: whether it works the same way each time. Robustness: whether it still works when things change. Predictability: whether you can tell in advance where it will fail. Safety: what happens when it does. “My model is 97% accurate” invites the only question that matters: what happens in the other 3%?

The answer to that question was the most useful idea of the week for anyone running agents in a regulated business: the error budget.

No AI system is perfect, so decide in advance how wrong it may be. At a 3% error budget, 10,000 customer tickets a month means 300 wrong resolutions. That is a number the business can see, price, and accept or reject. As one speaker put it, the space between the target and perfection is the budget you can spend on innovation. Stay within it and you keep shipping changes. Use it up and the work switches to reliability until you are back inside.

Start with the cost of an error

A separate session made the error budget practical. Its first question should open every AI discussion: what is the cost of an error?

Errors are inevitable. Every AI system works with incomplete data, ambiguous situations and a changing world, so some error can never be removed. What varies is what an error costs, and that depends on the use, not on the model.

An internal chatbot that gets something wrong costs an employee a few minutes of frustration, so one error in a hundred cases may be acceptable. A model that prices loans may cost a million dollars when it is wrong, so the acceptable rate is nearer one in ten thousand. Object detection in a self-driving car can cost lives, so the acceptable rate is nearer one in a hundred million. “99% accurate” means something very different in each case.

Different kinds of error cost different amounts too. In predictive maintenance, a false alarm costs an unnecessary inspection, and a missed alarm costs unplanned downtime. And there is always a baseline: AI usually replaces a process that already makes mistakes, so compare it with that process, not with perfection.

The session’s conclusion changes the business case. Most go and no-go decisions compare value with cost, where cost means the investment. The true cost of AI should also include the expected cost of its errors: how likely they are, multiplied by what they cost, before and after controls. Asking the question early avoids two opposite mistakes: dangerous under-engineering of high-stakes systems, and wasteful over-engineering of low-stakes ones.

Two warnings complete the picture. The first is the telephone game: chain three agents that are each 80% accurate and the whole system is right only 51% of the time, because errors compound at every handoff. The second is the outer learning loop: every time a person reviews an agent’s work, the system should get better for the next case, not just fix this one.

Autonomy becomes a dial

With an error budget in hand, autonomy stops being on or off. The sessions used slightly different names for the same ladder, gated, supervised, entrusted and autonomous, and the same rule: an agent moves up a level only when the evidence says it has earned it. That evidence rests on three things. Reliability: it does the right thing, repeatedly. Accountability: every action traces back to an owner. Security: it has only the access it needs, always enforced. The advice that surprised the room was to narrow the scope and widen the autonomy: give an agent a smaller job and far more freedom inside it.

The agent sessions described three kinds of agent, because each needs different controls. An assistant helps a person on request, and the person stays accountable for what they do with the output. A process agent runs a defined workflow end to end, with every step logged and exceptions sent to people. A role agent owns an outcome rather than a task, and needs a named human owner, an error budget and a kill switch. The best new term of the week was “human exceptioneering”: designing people’s work around the cases an agent cannot handle, rather than around checking the ones it can.

In the physical world, there is no undo

The physical AI session made all of this concrete in the most literal way. Its definition of physical AI came with a joke borrowed from a colleague: if you can throw it out of the window, it is physical AI. Robots, drones, autonomous vehicles and smart equipment that sense, move and act in the real world.

Its point was that the anatomy of a physical AI system, sensing, perceiving, thinking and acting, is the anatomy of a software agent. What changes is the cost of a mistake. A robot arm or a vehicle may need an error rate of 0.0001%. In the real world, there is no Control-Z.

The session drew one chart that I think every agent programme should copy:

Plot how much autonomy you have granted an agent against how reliable it actually is. Above the diagonal is what the session called the zone of unchecked optimism: more freedom than the evidence supports. Below it is untapped value: a reliable agent still on a short leash. The goal is to move along the diagonal, with autonomy following evidence. Its other advice transfers straight to software agents: augment people rather than replace them, start with the dull, dirty and dangerous work, and set strict business cases and clear lines of accountability before deployment, not after an incident.

Governing Actors, Not Systems: Zero Assumed Autonomy

Chapter 5 of 27 · 4 min read · 1 figure

The question this chapter answers

When software starts exercising authority, what does it take to govern it?

One infrastructure session described the change in three steps. Ordinary software takes instructions. Generative AI gives answers. Agentic AI exercises authority.

One sponsor reduced agent governance to four questions every CIO should be able to answer for every agent. Who or what is acting? What is it allowed to access, decide or change? Can we understand it and step in? Was the outcome worth what it cost?

Two of the governance warnings would interest any auditor. The first: what if everything works, and the outcome is still wrong? Every component reports green and the customer still got the wrong answer. The sessions answered with a chain of four responses, prevent, contain, reverse and compensate, and a principle: autonomy requires recoverability. The more consequential and irreversible an action, the less autonomy it gets without added control. The second warning, about agents quietly breaking the separation of duties, belongs to security and comes back in Part V.

Zero assumed autonomy

The session on the AI trust fabric gave this its sharpest form. Its starting point: AI is no longer advising, it is acting, and agent capability is growing faster than organisational trust. Agents multiply decisions faster than people can supervise them. Their autonomy is often unchecked and open-ended. And the conditions they act in keep changing. Gartner expects that by 2027, 40% of enterprises will demote or switch off autonomous agents because of governance gaps found only after incidents in production.

The session’s answer is a principle I think every board should adopt. Under zero assumed autonomy, no agent holds standing authority just because it was deployed, approved or trusted before. People grant autonomy, and the agent keeps earning it.

The session broke trust into six questions. Is the agent’s intent aligned with the enterprise’s? Does it have the authority? The right context? Enough confidence, given the risk? What oversight is appropriate? And can the outcome be proven afterwards? Its worked example was an agent paying supplier invoices:

Nothing about the agent changed between the two columns. The supplier’s bank details did. That one change in context lowered confidence, triggered human review and withdrew the agent’s authority automatically, with every step on record. That is the dial working as it should.

The session called the capability behind it a trust fabric: shared services that give every agent trusted context, delegated authority and adaptive oversight. Underneath sits a platform with an agent registry, a context layer, guardian agents that watch other agents, audit trails, exception handling and a kill switch. Its most memorable line: “Trust is a down payment on a future result.”

The 2027 technology trends made the urgency concrete. Gartner expects that by 2027, risk will force a rollback of 80% of autonomous AI, and that by 2029, agents will outpace 90% of the human-in-the-loop checks designed to supervise them. One of its top trends for 2027 responds directly: the agent coding harness, the layer that controls what coding agents see, use and produce. It supplies context, rules and guardrails, and tests and rejects flawed output automatically. The advice applies to every agent: treat the harness as a continuous discipline, and turn what works for one team into an enterprise standard.

Where the control point is moving

Software engineering is where this arrives first. In one migration shown on stage, an orchestrator ran up to 20 agents in parallel over 56 hours, ending in a single combined review. Writing code is no longer the bottleneck. Review is. The question for every engineering leader is whether the process is built for ten times more changes, or whether it will drown. Code review is becoming the control point for the whole software estate.

The pattern I found most interesting all week was the error budget escaping engineering. It started as a way to govern agents. Then it turned up in the boardroom as “how much security is enough?”. Then again in a session that applied reliability engineering to employees’ digital workplace, where a reliability target “made room for innovation”. An organisation that can say, for each critical service, how wrong it is willing to be and who agreed can govern AI, cyber and operations in one language. Most cannot yet. This is the argument I have made before as Autonomy as a Dial. The symposium gave it a budget.

The decisionEntry 05 · Ch. 5

Give every agent an identity, an owner, a cost line and an off switch, and make sure no agent holds standing authority that a change in conditions would not withdraw automatically.

Nowhere do these ideas meet harder reality than in banking, where an agent’s action can move money and one error can travel through the financial system.

Case Study, Agentic Banking: Trust as a Continuous Transaction

Chapter 6 of 27 · 4 min read · 1 figure

The question this chapter answers

What does earned autonomy look like in an industry where every action touches a ledger?

The banking session made a claim every bank board should hear: competition is shifting from providing financial products to owning the trusted orchestration of a customer’s financial outcomes. Customers increasingly start their journeys somewhere else, and an AI agent may decide which provider wins. In that world, the bank that arranges savings, credit, insurance and investments on the customer’s behalf, including products it does not make, holds the relationship. 78% of banking CIOs say their organisations have deployed or will deploy AI agents by 2027.

The session drew a spectrum: from human-led banking, through AI-assisted and AI-enabled, to AI-led and finally autonomous banking, where people govern intent, risk and ethics and the systems optimise themselves. Then it placed banking workflows on it:

The logic is simple once drawn. Where decisions are well defined, such as onboarding checks or payment processing, AI can act on its own within limits set in advance. Where decisions rest on judgement, such as corporate lending or dispute resolution, AI informs and people decide. In between sits an expansion zone, where AI leads and people oversee.

The advice on where to begin was to design for the enterprise rather than for separate use cases, in three steps. First, decide which workflows you delegate and which you control. Second, decide how people and agents work together: orchestrator agents, business agents, data agents and evaluator agents in layers, with people holding decision authority. Only then, third, decide which systems, data platforms, models and policies you need.

The cases showed what earned autonomy looks like. A large European bank’s car-loan workflow uses agents to gather and organise application data, and a policy agent to approve, decline or pass to a person, with confidence thresholds, role-based access and every step logged. Processing time fell from two days to 15 minutes, accuracy rose from 92% to 94%, cost per loan more than halved, and 27 employees moved to other roles. A bank serving 100 million users built its service agents in four phases: ground them in trusted knowledge, make their actions safe and auditable, measure and improve, and only then allow them to resolve cases on their own. Customer satisfaction rose 37 percentage points. Another bank’s self-healing engineering platform has found more than 12,800 issues and generated more than 8,100 code fixes, each still reviewed and approved by a person.

Ambition is running ahead of that discipline. Among banks deploying agents, 43% aim within three years for agents that coordinate many tasks on their own inside one application, and 19% for agents that collaborate across applications with no human involved.

When it goes wrong

The banking trends session described failure under a heading I have not been able to forget: algorithmic contagion. Agentic workflows have no walls to contain them. One small logic error does not stay where it started. It travels at machine speed from core ledgers to payment rails, clearing houses and liquidity pools, growing as it goes.

Its picture of the insider threat was just as clear. A firewall protects the edge of the network, but an agent already has direct connections to the core ledger. A compromised agent can do damage with perfectly valid credentials. In the session’s words, the enemy holds the keys to the vault.

Hence its conclusion: in agentic banking, trust becomes a continuous transaction, checked at every step rather than granted once at the door. Zero-trust architecture, which checks every user and every agent at each step and stops a compromised agent moving freely across systems, is moving from good practice to precondition. 62% of banking CIOs have deployed, or will deploy within a year, advanced authentication technologies.

Map your workflows onto the control, expansion and scaling zones. Start with well-defined work, and keep decisions that rest on judgement under human control until the evidence says otherwise.

There is a limit to what any dial can fix. An agent can only act as well as it understands the business it is acting in. Which brings us to the scarcest input of all.

Context Is the Moat

Chapter 7 of 27 · 8 min read · 2 figures

The question this chapter answers

If everyone can rent the same intelligence, what makes yours better?

One sponsor gave the clearest picture of the week. Ask an agent to “send me a pizza” and there are four places it can fail. The model turns your words into instructions. The tools place the order. The data knows you are a customer. The context knows it is Friday night, the film is about to start, and you always order pepperoni.

The data track put a number on it: agent accuracy and cost are 75% context and 25% data. Reasoning models working through unstructured information burn seven to ten times the tokens. Well-structured context cuts token use by two to three times, with the same or better accuracy. One session showed a context graph lifting agent accuracy by about 40%.

The difference between data and context is easiest to see in a single question. “How many of our clients are over 50?” is a query. “How many of our clients will retire in the next 15 years?” needs interpretation: retirement rules, account details, regulation and time. Only 7% of organisations say their data is completely ready for AI, and Gartner expects 60% of projects without AI-ready data to be abandoned by the end of 2026.

Agents fail on context, not on data

The session on data and analytics capabilities for AI put this in one slide title I wrote down word for word: “AI agents don’t fail on data. They fail on context.” Raw data has no meaning on its own. A context layer, made of semantics, metadata, knowledge graphs, lineage and policy, turns enterprise data into something an agent can reason about.

Gartner’s way of drawing this is what it calls the Intelligence Capabilities Framework. I found it the most useful map of the week for explaining to a non-technical executive where context sits:

Read it from the bottom. Information is the enterprise’s data, structured and unstructured, organised so it can be found and governed. Context sits on top of it and gives it meaning: what “customer”, “revenue” or “exposure” means here, how things connect, where each fact came from and what rules apply. Intelligence is the techniques: analytics, machine learning, agents and decision models. Interaction is how people and systems use it: dashboards, conversations, APIs. Outcomes are the decisions and the value. Trust and governance wrap the whole stack.

The point of the picture is the order. Most AI programmes invest at the intelligence layer and hope the layers below will cope. The session’s first action for a CIO on a Monday morning was to run a reality check of the context layer.

What a system of context is

The emerging name for that layer is a system of context. The term is about to be used everywhere, so it helps to be precise about it.

Think of what a good relationship manager knows after ten years with a client. Who the client is, and how they connect to other people and companies. What happened last time, and why. And what the words mean in this business: that “exposure” means one thing here and something else there, that a particular limit has an exception, and who approved it.

A system of context captures those three things for machines. Unified data about the entities that matter. The history of interactions and decisions. And the semantics that give both their meaning, often called an ontology. It sits beneath the applications rather than inside any one of them, so every agent, model and screen above it draws on the same understanding.

In one demonstration, the same renewal question went to the same four data sources twice. With the ontology, the agent searched all four, flagged 13 accounts with clear actions and used up to 25% fewer tokens. Without it, the agent checked two sources at random, flagged 23 accounts, ten of them wrongly, and needed a person to nudge it. Good context made the agent both more accurate and cheaper to run. Chapter 9 shows, through the application everyone assumed would hold this knowledge, why it has to be a layer of its own.

Dirty data used to produce a wrong report. Now it produces a wrong action.

The banking trends session called this the memory liability. People treat data as a historical record to review and argue about. An agent treats the data lake as its living memory and acts on whatever is in it, immediately. That is why Gartner expects 70% of banks to run key data as managed data products by 2027: trusted, reusable data assets with owners, built for outcomes such as client onboarding.

The best case of the week was a large insurer that turned millions of legal rulings into a knowledge graph. Legal research became six times faster and claims handling three times faster. More striking, the variation in payouts fell by two-thirds, from 30% to 10%. Context did more than speed up the work. It made the organisation consistent with itself.

Another session named the root problem: AI inherited the task and the language, but not the judgement behind them. That is the argument at the centre of The Empty Window. The judgement that makes a business work sits with the people who run it, and it was never written down at a level of detail an agent can use.

Governing what agents read

If context is what agents act on, governing it becomes a business control rather than a data team’s chore. The data governance session opened with a warning I expect to hear repeated: data governance will be the single point of failure for organisations’ AI ambitions.

Its picture of the change was clear. Today, governance is mostly written down and relies on people reading the policy and following it. In future, governance is enforced by the technology itself, at the moment data is used: a privacy check in the code, masking applied automatically, access refused if a request crosses a border it should not. The session called this policy as code. What gets governed widens too, from tables and reports to AI models, agents, data products, unstructured content and the decisions people and agents make together.

The idea I liked most was the trust model. Rather than calling data simply “governed” or “not governed”, rank each source on a graduated scale, from trusted and assured, through proven and acknowledged, down to untrusted or unknown. Then let the trust level decide who can use the data and for what, and let it change as quality, lineage and users’ experience change. For AI agents, the session added trust measures of their own: time to trust, adaptive behaviour, and cost and value.

The people who look after data change with it. The session’s phrase was that the data steward moves from “reluctant data janitor” to “valued gig worker”: from policing and chasing exceptions by hand to coaching, judging context in real time and working alongside agents that automate the routine checks. And it ended with a warning that applies to this whole chapter: beware of agent-washing in governance as well.

Most of what agents need is unstructured

There is a practical problem underneath all of this. Between 70% and 90% of the data in an enterprise is unstructured: emails, documents, contracts, call recordings, images, web pages, sensor logs. It is growing by 40% to 60% a year, and it is exactly what generative AI and agents read. The unstructured data session’s title said what to do first: govern it before you try to unlock its value.

Its method had five steps, with metadata running underneath all of them:

Discover and catalogue: find the data, starting with what matters most to the business, and record basic metadata such as type, location, owner and dates. Preprocess and analyse: clean it, standardise it, find sensitive information such as personal data, and mask what must be masked. Tag and classify: label it by sensitivity, topic, business domain and version, starting with a simple scheme. Connect and share: link related content, often through a knowledge graph in which a customer places an order, a contract entitles the customer to support, and a support call relates to a product manual. And define and enforce policies throughout: storage, life cycle, privacy, access and security.

The warning that stayed with me: without a full catalogue with good metadata, an organisation will miss information that a regulator may audit, or that an AI system needs. Metadata is what turns a pile of documents into context.

Meanwhile the models are becoming interchangeable

Another sponsor session opened with the question every strategy now has to answer: if everyone can access the same intelligence, what will set you apart? More than 40 frontier models arrived in a few months, from many labs, with prices falling at each release. The model market, as the speaker put it, “became a portfolio, not a hierarchy”. The practical question for a CIO is no longer which model is best. It is whether every task goes to the most cost-effective model that can do it.

The session’s answer to its own question, lightly paraphrased: models commoditise, and your own intelligence compounds. It called the asset token capital: the AI capability a firm builds and owns, as opposed to the model it licenses. And it offered three questions to put to any AI platform. Does it ground answers in our data, with the permissions we already have? Does it reach both business knowledge and engineering knowledge? Do our context, our memory and our quality evaluations stay ours?

Gartner’s seven-themes session said the same in its own words: business context is a competitive moat, and agents need precise instructions, because vague intent produces confident mistakes. The session on competitive moats showed where this goes. Expertise, one of the oldest sources of advantage, is moving out of people’s heads and into a shared layer that every agent can draw on. Whoever builds that layer first keeps the moat. One slide simply said: memory as moat.

Give your context layer an owner, a budget and a freshness standard, rank your sources by how far agents may trust them, and treat context as the one asset every agent you deploy will share.

Build or Buy, Rewritten: Who Controls the Intelligence

Chapter 8 of 27 · 4 min read · 1 figure

The question this chapter answers

When the model is a commodity, what should you build, what should you buy, and what must you never give away?

The banking session drew the change as two lists. In the world we knew, we asked whether a platform had the features we needed, whether it was secure and compliant, configurable, affordable and easy to integrate. In the world we now live in, the questions are different. Where does the context live? Where is the decision logic written? Who controls the orchestration? Who controls how it evolves? What happens if we want to change vendors?

Look at what lands in the build quadrant. Not the most technically complex work, but the work that carries the bank’s own judgement: how it prices, what it offers next, how much risk it will take. Those decisions are what make one bank different from another. Banking IT leaders expect a balanced mix of building and buying to stay the most common approach through 2030, which makes the line between the quadrants a strategic choice rather than a procurement one.

The session also gave a maturity test with an acronym, G.U.I.D.E., for what an organisation must be able to do as AI moves from assisting to acting:

The rule that comes with it: your least mature capability limits how much authority you can give AI. A bank with excellent orchestration and weak evidence cannot run autonomous actions, however good its models are.

The application estate is being redrawn

Gartner’s framework splits the application estate into three layers. The system of record is the ledger that must never be wrong: core banking, ERP, the customer master. The system of intelligence is the models and analytics that advise. The system of action is where people, software and agents do the work. The rule: buy the record, build the edges.

That rule matters because software itself is changing shape. About $300 billion was wiped off software and data stocks on fears that AI will replace it. The picture presented was more careful. Enterprise applications are not dying, at least through 2032, but they are sinking into the stack as plumbing, with no screen of their own and agents as the interface. Vendors know it. Expect renewal increases of 20% to 40%, AI bundles that lift prices by 30% to 60%, and new charges for any agent, yours or someone else’s, that touches their system of record. As screens disappear, whoever controls the schema, the structure of your data, controls the relationship. Data portability, schema ownership and exit terms belong in every contract now.

ERP is following the same path. Gartner’s picture of its successor is cloud-native, built from parts that can be swapped, and improving itself, with the user experience as a separate layer. Most organisations are far from it. The practical route is a sidecar: connect AI layers to the existing ERP rather than rip it out. Buy for foundational capabilities, blend for differentiating ones and build for innovative ones. One automotive supplier consolidated more than 100 manufacturing applications into one or two, cut feature lead times from weeks to under a day, and reduced the cost per connected asset by 80%.

The predictions session went a step further. By 2029, it expects 80% of new applications to be intentionally disposable: used for less than a year and then replaced. Why upgrade, it asked, when you can rip and replace for free? Then it added that this is not free. The advice is to focus on the cost and speed of creating and retiring applications rather than on modernising them. Put that forecast beside the context argument and the logic is plain. When the software on top becomes disposable, the lasting asset is the knowledge underneath it.

Before you sign any AI or application contract, know where your context, policies and decision logic will live, and make sure the data, the schema and the exit stay yours.

Case Study, The Hollowing Out of CRM

Chapter 9 of 27 · 9 min read · 4 figures

The question this chapter answers

What happens to the system every customer-facing team lives in, once agents rather than people become its main users?

For twenty years, customer relationship management has been where sales, service and marketing teams spend their day. The session on the next three years of CRM described four forces pulling it apart.

Customers need you less. 45% of business buyers used independent AI tools in a recent purchase, mostly to research vendors and products, and 59% will contact a sales representative only after they have worked out the options themselves.

Consumers trust AI more than brands. 71% say they relied on generative AI recommendations to make decisions, and 47% would seriously consider buying from an unfamiliar brand if an AI tool recommended it.

Customer data is disappearing. When a personal agent does the browsing, journey steps vanish, demographic and behavioural signals go missing, and more of the engagement is triggered by agents.

And AI can now serve customers who were never worth serving. Small field-service firms used to miss up to a fifth of their inbound leads. One start-up’s agents, trained on the trades, now answer calls, texts and chats around the clock and book 90% of jobs without a person involved.

The architecture is shifting in three steps. In the CRM era, people open the CRM and work in its screens. In the transition, AI opens the CRM on their behalf, through conversation. In the AI era, the CRM becomes a component, and eventually a skill: a set of tools that a frontier model, an industry model or a collaboration tool calls when needed, while the user never sees a CRM screen at all.

New challengers are coming from three directions. Model-centric players offer CRM as a skill of a frontier or industry model. AI-native CRMs make the agent the product. And enterprises can build their own, because the cost of building has collapsed. The session’s best line was about that last option.

Generating an application is cheap. Owning, securing and running it is not. Meanwhile, in the speaker’s words, vendors sprint and customers jog: vendors are shipping assistants and agents faster than customers can absorb them.

So CRM is not so much phased out as hollowed out. The record survives, as plumbing. Everything that made the CRM the centre of gravity moves. The screen moves to surfaces you may not control. The workflow moves to agents. And the understanding of the customer moves to a system of context that should never have been locked inside one vendor’s application.

Why CRM has to change, not just add AI

CRM was built as a system of record for relationships, designed around a person at a screen: a salesperson logging a call, a service agent updating a case, a marketer building a segment. Its value, and its price, came from three things bundled together. The record: who the customer is and what happened. The workflow: the steps people follow. And the screen: where they follow them. Vendors charged per seat because value grew with the number of people using the screens.

Agentic AI unbundles all three, for five reasons.

  1. The person at the screen stops being the main user. First an assistant opens the CRM for the employee. Then an agent acts without anyone opening it. Then the customer’s own agent arrives from outside. Per-seat pricing stops making sense when the busiest user is software, which is why platforms are introducing consumption charges for agents that touch their records.
  2. The workflow moves into orchestration. A real customer journey crosses sales, service, billing, risk and operations. Agents acting across a journey have to coordinate many systems, so the CRM becomes one tool among several rather than the place where the process lives.
  3. The customer stops showing up where the CRM can see them. When research and comparison happen inside a personal AI, the journey steps the CRM used to capture never arrive. Understanding the customer now depends on what you can assemble from every interaction you do have.
  4. Building has become cheap. If a team can generate a working sales or service tool in days, the incumbent’s features stop being a moat. What stays hard, and valuable, is custody: securing, evaluating and maintaining what was generated, and the knowledge it runs on.
  5. The vendors are going different ways. The session described at least six distinct strategies among the large incumbents, from wrapping AI around the existing CRM, to turning it into a control tower for customer operations, to rebuilding inbound sales from scratch. New features arrive faster than most customers can take them in.

What an agentic CRM looks like underneath

The session’s reference architecture shows where the value is moving.

The core CRM is still there, holding sales, service, marketing and commerce records. Around it sit three things that hardly mattered before. An agent framework that runs agents, keeps track of their work, calls tools and hands off to people. An integration layer of APIs, events, connectors and a gateway through which agents, including outside ones, reach tools. And a band of agent governance: a control plane, identities for agents, monitoring, evaluations, guardrails and a registry of AI assets.

Look at one line inside the agent framework: context grounding. It is the step where an agent fetches what it needs to know before it acts. The framework lives in the CRM platform, but the context does not. It sits in data lakes, customer data platforms, master data and knowledge bases across the enterprise. That gap, between where agents act and where meaning lives, is what a system of context closes.

The system of context, laid out

It is easy to confuse the system of context with things most enterprises already own. A CRM stores records of relationships and the processes around them. A customer data platform unifies profile and behaviour data, mostly for marketing. Master data management keeps one authoritative version of key entities such as customers and products. A data lake stores almost everything, raw. Each is useful. None of them, on its own, tells an agent who this customer is to the business right now, what has happened across every channel, and which rules apply.

It has four parts. Entities and relationships: the customer, their accounts, products, people and companies, and how they connect. Interaction history: what happened across every channel, including decisions taken and the reasons for them, which is the memory an agent otherwise lacks. Business semantics: what terms, products and rules mean in this organisation. And controls: permissions, so an agent sees only what the person it acts for may see; provenance, so every answer can be traced to its source; and freshness, because context decays as policies change, products launch and people leave.

An illustration of my own shows why no single application can hold it. Imagine a corporate client asking, through its own treasury agent, why a payment was held. The answer is spread across the payments system, the sanctions screening record, the relationship manager’s notes, the client’s mandate and the bank’s policy. No CRM holds all of that. A system of context can join it, apply the right permissions, and let a service agent explain the hold, or pass it to a person, with the evidence attached.

The session’s roadmap gives the order of work. Start from the critical customer journeys, not from the data estate. Identify the context that is unique to you. Separate it from the CRM, so the schema and the meaning belong to you rather than to an application you may replace. Capture interaction history and decision records as a matter of course. Enforce permissions at the moment an agent retrieves. Expose context to agents through a governed gateway. And measure its coverage, accuracy and freshness the way you measure any other critical service.

What changes for the people who run it

The CRM team changes with it. Today it asks whether the system is configured correctly, trains users on new screens, is paid per feature and per person-day, and governs the build: build, test, go live, done. In three years it will ask whether the system is performing, run evaluations, context standards, an agent registry and token budgets, and govern both the build and the running system, because going live is the start, not the end.

The session made the autonomy dial concrete for customer work, with three modes. In automated mode, actions approved in advance by policy run at scale, with thresholds, rollback and an accountable owner watching the numbers. In supervised mode, the agent prepares the context and the proposed action, and a person checks the reasoning and approves it. In human-only mode, kept for high ambiguity, empathy or legal liability, AI only gathers and summarises, and the owner makes every decision. Ownership has to be mapped to specific actions, such as launching a campaign or issuing a refund, with a named job title. “Marketing owns the AI” is not ownership. In the session’s words: maximum autonomy is not the objective; appropriate autonomy is.

The human role shifts from fixer to supervisor. A fixer patches broken outputs out of sight, the reasons go unrecorded, and the hidden workload breeds burnout and scepticism about AI. A supervisor applies judgement where context, empathy and relationships matter, and every intervention is tracked and used to improve the system. One large furniture retailer showed what redeploying people can be worth. After its chatbot absorbed millions of routine queries, it retrained thousands of call-centre staff as remote interior design advisers. The chatbot saved about €13 million. The new advisory service brought in about €1.3 billion in revenue.

What the symposium proved, and what it left open

Put these sessions side by side and the industry has arrived, from several directions at once, at a claim I have made before: the value of a deployed AI system depends on the quality of the knowledge layer beneath it, not on the model within it. The Layers You Cannot Buy argued that models, tooling and protocols are maturing and can be bought, while your context and your controls cannot. The symposium agreed.

What it did not supply was the meter. Everyone called context critical infrastructure. Nobody said whose budget it sits in, who keeps it fresh, or how to measure its quality. As I argued in The Search Tax, an agent that keeps searching is telling you the knowledge beneath it is thin. A moat you cannot measure is a moat you cannot fund.

The closest thing to an answer came from Gartner’s top strategic technology trends for 2027, which named the AI context platform as one of them: a layer that turns knowledge into context an AI can read, connects meaning and history through ontologies, and combines semantics, operational state and provenance into a single source of truth for people and AI alike. Its advice: build shared context now, before scaling agents; avoid lock-in by building in modules; and hold business owners accountable for the quality of their context. That names an owner. The trust model from the governance session offers the start of a measure. The budget is still missing.

Sovereignty as Optionality

Chapter 10 of 27 · 5 min read

The question this chapter answers

How do you keep control of your technology when the world around it fragments?

One story from the cloud sessions changed how I think about data rules. A bank had followed a strict rule to keep all its data in the country, with backups only in domestic data centres. Then physical strikes disabled the local backup sites. To keep the bank running, regulators waived their own rules so operations could be moved abroad.

The speaker’s lesson: sovereignty is a design objective, and resilience is a survival requirement. When the two collide, survival wins. It is far better to have designed for that moment than to improvise it.

For anyone reading from the Gulf, this part of the symposium was not hypothetical. The continuity sessions drew directly on lessons from the war in the Middle East: concentration in cloud and AI, regulators waiving data rules in the middle of a crisis, and banks running on a minimum footing. Resilience stopped being a slide about what could happen and became a slide about what did.

The AI supply chain has its own version of this. Access to a model can be withdrawn, repriced or restricted by events no customer controls, from a provider’s own outage to a new law. Europe’s AI Act, enforced by national authorities from August, is one example of the rules changing underneath a deployment.

Location becomes a source of options, not a place

The cloud sessions argued that the era of the single cloud supplier is ending. By 2031, more than 70% of enterprises are expected to run intensive model work in one cloud using data held in another, and the average enterprise will use 50% more cloud vendors than today. The trend, as one speaker put it, is not a mass return from the cloud. It is some return of AI workloads, and sometimes “geopatriation”: moving workloads back to a home or trusted region for geopolitical reasons. The advice: stop thinking about location as a place, and start thinking about it as a source of options. The geography of greatest risk is wherever a critical business capability has a single point of failure, not wherever the servers happen to sit.

The government sessions added a useful warning against sovereignty washing. Hosted in the region is not the same as sovereign. Sovereign is not the same as secure. Compliant is not the same as sovereign. And the session on volatility added the line that turns this from a principle into a budget item: optionality must be pre-funded to be real. An exit plan with no money behind it is a wish.

In banking the shift is already visible. 51% of banking technology leaders say data and technology sovereignty is a top priority, fully built into their AI strategy, and none said it was not a priority at all. Gartner expects that by 2028, 47% of banking workloads on public cloud will run on a sovereign cloud, often operated by domestic or regional providers.

Sovereign technical debt

The most practical tool came from the European sovereignty session, and it applies anywhere regulators expect sensitive data and operations to stay under national control. It started by rejecting a myth: sovereignty is not an emotional decision. It shows up as cost increases, availability problems and confidentiality risk. What has changed is the goal. Between 2000 and 2023, sovereignty mostly meant data protection. Since 2023 it has meant technological autonomy: protecting the business from geopolitical risk.

A fully sovereign technology stack is not a realistic goal today. The realistic goal is to reduce and spread what the session called sovereign technical debt: the risk that builds up when a foreign government has influence over your technology suppliers. Like financial debt, you cannot clear it overnight, but you can measure it, pay it down and avoid concentrating it.

The method is concrete. Score every vendor across eight dimensions, from legal jurisdiction and data to operations, supply chain and security, each on a scale of zero to four. Require level three or above for critical systems and level two for the rest. Spread what remains across countries, because diversity breeds resilience. Then work in three-year sprints, with no complex migration in the first 18 months. The speaker warned that the programme will probably outlast several CIOs.

The AI-specific warning was easy to miss. Bought AI carries hidden concentration risk: your vendors’ products often depend on the same few underlying models, a dependency you may not discover until it fails. Ask for options, or the ability to bring your own model. Built AI needs model agility: test and secure several models, and route work between them.

Durable control, portable execution

That routing is where governance becomes architecture. There will be no single AI stack. Frontier, enterprise, small and specialist models will run across public cloud, private cloud, mainframes and the edge. The infrastructure sessions’ answer was a control plane: one logical layer where policy flows down and evidence flows up, while each platform keeps its own native controls. A request is routed to a private small model, an enterprise model or a frontier model according to the class of its data, its sovereignty needs, its risk, its cost and how fast it needs an answer.

Open-weight models deployed privately can cut AI costs by up to 90% for the right workloads, which makes the router, not any one model, the strategic asset. The market makes routing easier every month. Open-weight models, whose parameters are published so they can run on your own infrastructure, held nine of the top ten places on one widely followed model leaderboard in September 2026, and some run on ordinary hardware, even gaming graphics cards. They bring their own risks, from data leakage and security to bias and regulation, which is why they belong in a controlled environment with guardrails on what goes in and what comes out.

The 2027 technology trends take the idea to its end with sovereign tech: a whole technology stack designed so you can leave any vendor, because regulators increasingly require exit strategies and AI turns platform dependence into an economic issue. The advice: demand credible exit and replacement plans, judge sovereignty workload by workload, and track the alliances that govern access to cloud, AI and chips.

The session’s closing line is the one I would give every board: sovereignty is not achieved through one dramatic migration. It is built through thousands of deliberate decisions that give the business more options and fewer single points of geopolitical failure.

The Minimum Viable Organisation

Chapter 11 of 27 · 2 min read · 1 figure

The question this chapter answers

What must keep running when everything else stops, and have you rehearsed it?

The continuity session’s main point was that extreme disruption rarely comes from one failure. It comes when several comfortable assumptions fail together. “Our cloud makes us resilient.” “This is a trusted supplier.” “We’ve backed up our SaaS data.” “Our people are trained to spot deepfakes.” “We are in a safe geography.” Each failure is survivable alone. Geopolitical turmoil, concentration risk, regulatory complexity, supply-chain disruption, cyber conflict and AI-enabled deception arriving together are not. 62% of heads of enterprise risk admit their continuity plans do not prepare them well enough.

The answer is a minimum viable organisation: the smallest set of functions you must keep running in a crisis, decided in advance.

The actions were specific. Map your dependencies, single points of failure and how far damage would spread. Find hidden concentrations, including your suppliers’ suppliers. Keep backups that cannot be altered, and simple manual alternatives. Rebuild in clean rooms, isolated environments known to be safe, so you do not restore the infection along with the data. Recover identity first, because nothing else can be trusted until you know who is who. Be ready to cut off a third party. And accept that after a long crisis, the goal may be a “disrupted steady state” rather than a return to normal.

The more hopeful argument was that AI’s most strategic value may turn out to be resilience rather than productivity. Gartner expects organisations that apply AI to resilience to get at least 25% more return on their AI investment by 2028: spotting risk earlier, reacting faster, simulating how to adapt, and learning after every event. The aim is to come out of each shock stronger, not just to survive it.

Tier your services before the crisis, rehearse running the minimum viable business with your main cloud provider unavailable, and recover identity first.

The Attack Surface Now Acts

Chapter 13 of 27 · 7 min read · 2 figures

The question this chapter answers

What has AI really changed about the threat, and how do you secure software that makes its own decisions?

The cybersecurity radar session opened with a slide that asked: AI apocalypse, or marketing? The next slide answered: it doesn’t matter. Boards are asking either way, and technology leaders need answers now.

What I liked about the session was its honesty about the evidence. Most AI-augmented attacks today are tactical and opportunistic rather than transformational. Attackers mostly use AI to do familiar things faster and more convincingly.

The picture splits in two. What is already observed in the wild sits at the lower end of sophistication: better phishing and social engineering, deepfake impersonation, malicious language models rented as a service, help writing malware, and hijacked AI accounts. What is anticipated sits higher up: automated attack chains, offensive models trained for the job, tools that find unknown vulnerabilities, and agentic attack frameworks. The early signals of that second group are already here. The session cited a single attacker using two AI platforms to take data from nine government agencies, malware written by casual “vibe coding”, and malware that calls AI at the moment it runs.

The clock has collapsed. The average time from a vulnerability becoming known to its exploitation went from 2.3 years in 2018 to 21.5 days in 2025 to eight hours in mid-2026. A patching process measured in weeks no longer matches the threat. One of Gartner’s top technology trends for 2027 responds to this: autonomous exposure remediation, in which AI finds, tests, fixes and if necessary rolls back vulnerabilities end to end, to close an average exposure gap of 50 days.

The defender still has the advantage

The session’s most useful message was a hopeful one. This technology does not only scale attackers. It scales defenders too. Automation is a better business case for a defender, who runs the same estate every day, than for an attacker. Defenders know the terrain. Information sharing between defenders is getting faster, while turning a new AI capability into a weapon still takes attackers time. In an AI world, the speaker argued, costs favour defenders. In his words, this is a fight we can win.

The weak point is still people. The session showed how the threats aimed at people have multiplied across every channel: fake job posts, fake employees, attacks that bombard people with login approvals until they tap yes, help desks talked into resetting accounts, deepfake voice and video calls, payroll fraud. Many of them are directly accelerated by AI.

Shadow AI: this time it acts

Here is the number that should make every CIO look again. In Gartner’s employee research, 57% use personal generative AI accounts for work, 33% admit putting sensitive work information into public or unapproved AI tools, and 36% download or use unapproved AI applications on their work devices.

One session gave this its best title: shadow AI is the new shadow IT, but this time it acts. We have seen the pattern before. Ten years ago, teams signed up for software with a credit card, the service desk could not support it, and the licence bill came as a surprise. Today it is personal AI accounts, a service desk that cannot answer AI questions, and token costs that change with every model.

The difference is what the tool can do. With software as a service, a person decides and the tool carries it out. With an agent, the software decides and carries it out. It runs workflows and grants access. No person is in every step. One error can repeat a thousand times. The same input can produce a different output. And the models underneath keep changing. Shadow IT leaked data. Shadow AI takes action.

The session’s good news was that most organisations already have the controls they need, in the IT service management practices they have run for years.

Practice you already run Applied to AI agents
Change management Levels of autonomy: what an agent may do alone and what needs approval
Approval matrices Tiered authority, based on impact and risk
Configuration management Register every agent as a configuration item, with an owner, a scope and permissions
Service level management Accountability for the outcomes agents deliver
Incident and problem management A clear path when an agent fails, and a root-cause analysis afterwards

Its worked example was an agent that handles access requests, with four levels. At level one it suggests: it recommends a role, and a person decides and acts. At level two it prepares and waits: for access to a finance system, a person approves. At level three it acts and reports: for standard software from the catalogue, a person reviews samples. At level four it acts alone within guardrails: for a password reset, the person owns the guardrails. What decides the level is the sensitivity of the data, the level of privilege, whether the action can be reversed, and the cost. And the answer to “who is accountable?” was plain: the service owner. The agent executes. A person owns.

The opening keynote put the same idea in sharper terms. Treat shadow AI as crime prevention, with guardian agents watching other agents in depth, rather than as a policy document.

Securing agents: identity, least agency and intent

The session on AI agent security made the controls specific. It started by sorting agents by use, because each kind carries different risks. Customer-facing agents are exposed to the outside world, so the risks are brand damage, fraud and data loss. Back-end worker agents need their own credentials, and the risk is corrupted data. Agents that work inside an employee’s own workspace act with that person’s identity. Coding agents run in development environments, where the risks are abuse of permissions and an agent drifting from what it was asked to do.

It then named the new kinds of attack that only exist because agents exist: prompts written to manipulate the agent, poisoned memory, an agent given more power than its task needs, and compromised components in the agent’s own supply chain.

Its programme had five workstreams. Find every agent, across every channel through which agents arrive. Model what each one can access. Give each the least agency it needs: only the actions it needs, only when it needs them. Manage its exposures. And monitor intent, so you notice when an agent drifts into the wrong tool or the wrong action.

Three details stood out. Every agent should have its own identity. Acting “on behalf of” a user should be reserved for special cases, such as customer-facing agents, and access should be granted just in time. The most common mistakes are still basic: agent interfaces that anyone can call without signing in, and agents reaching resources they were never meant to touch. Second, fixing an agent’s prompt is not the same as fixing the agent. An AI application can repeat the same vulnerable behaviour after its prompt has been hardened, so some exposures need a runtime guardrail as well as a patch. Third, multi-agent systems can quietly break the separation of duties. If one agent can approve and another can pay, an orchestrator over both has indirect access to the whole transaction, which no single human employee would ever be given.

The radar session gave a simple way to decide how tightly to govern an agent, based on what it can do:

The more an AI can change, the more controlled its zone. AI that only produces content, without needing privileged data, can be governed lightly. AI that can run programs or issue commands to other computers needs more. AI that can change a system of record, or something in the physical world, needs more again. And ecosystems of AI that can do that together need the most. The same agent technology can sit in any zone. What it is allowed to touch decides the zone.

Gartner’s predictions show why this matters now. By 2028, it expects a quarter of breaches to trace back to agents. By 2030, it expects 80% of organisations with public-facing AI to have suffered a cost exhaustion attack, in which an attacker deliberately runs up the bill. Token spend is becoming a security signal as well as a budget line. And AI agent identity is one of the top technology trends for 2027, because identity controls built for people are the wrong shape for software that needs a unique, narrow identity for each task.

The session’s action plan is worth copying as it stands:

Next 90 days Define an agent security policy and access model. Fix the simple misconfigurations. Put deterministic safeguards in at runtime.
Next 12 months Run adversarial prompt testing on high-risk agents. Add supply-chain controls specific to agents. Enforce least privilege that adjusts as tasks change, and detect when an agent’s intent drifts.

How Much Security Is Enough?

Chapter 14 of 27 · 7 min read · 1 figure

The question this chapter answers

How do you give a board confidence in security, when fewer breaches will never be enough to earn it?

90% of non-executive directors lack confidence in the value of their cybersecurity spend. The board session on cyber opened with that number and then explained why the usual reporting makes it worse.

Most technology leaders survive the board meeting. They report regulatory compliance, attack volumes and the project queue, answer questions and leave. The session asked them to do something else: become what Gartner calls a sense-maker. A sense-maker puts security into business terms and gets the board to agree on two things, the level of protection and the cost.

The picture is a grid. One axis is the level of protection, the other is the cost of security, each low, just right or high. The goal is to get the board to agree that both are just right, and to know why. Everything else in the session was about how to get there. It asked technology leaders to answer one simple question: how much security is enough?

That is the error budget from Part II, arriving in the boardroom.

Simplify: show what the board gets for its money

The first advice was to stop presenting threats and maturity scores. “We moved from a maturity level of 2.4 to 2.7” means nothing to a director. “We cut the time our systems are open to attack by half” does. So does a sentence about ransomware: we cannot control whether we get hit, but we do control how fast we recover, and here is our plan.

Gartner’s answer to “what should we measure?” is a set of outcome-driven metrics, each one describing a level of protection the business can see and buy more or less of. Among them: how long it takes to contain and to fix an incident; how often ransomware recovery is rehearsed; how much of the estate has multifactor login, privileged access controls and endpoint protection; how quickly access is removed when someone leaves; how many suppliers have never been assessed; how much shadow IT there is; and how many AI systems have had a risk assessment. One director’s comment summed up the alternative: don’t just show up with a red, amber and green dashboard or the number of incidents blocked.

Speak candidly: start a fire, but bring water

Exposing uncomfortable facts builds board confidence. The session’s example: “Our incident remediation time is 22 hours. Our peers’ is nine.” That starts the fire. Then bring the water: “We should invest this much to bring remediation down to nine hours.” Or, when no comparison is available: we can be hacked at any time, and we need to invest this much to be able to defend the decisions we made.

One finding surprised me. Directors defer to fellow board members’ expertise on cyber risk more than on any other topic: 42% often or very often, ahead of regulatory compliance at 41%, technology at 29% and corporate finance at 28%. The session treated that deference as a hidden risk in itself. If one director “does cyber” and the rest nod, the board is not overseeing anything. The fix is to invite questions, ask directors what concerns them most, and make clear that scepticism and curiosity are signs of good oversight.

Align value: protect what the organisation cares about

When Gartner asked directors what most increases their confidence in security, the top answer was alignment with business impact, at 24%, followed by external validation and frameworks at 21%. Transparency was named by only 8%, which I read as a sign of how rarely boards get it rather than how little they value it.

The point underneath: fewer breaches will not earn board trust. Consistent performance is expected, not rewarded. Business alignment is what earns trust. And CEOs rank growth and cost far above risk among their priorities. So security should be framed in their terms. For revenue: we help the business enter new markets by making distributor partnerships secure. For cost: AI risk assessments let us develop faster and more cheaply. For risk: we are preparing for post-quantum encryption to keep our data safe.

Give the board time, and talk between meetings

55% of directors say they want more time for discussion in board meetings. The session’s suggestion was to present for seven minutes and discuss for eight, and to talk about one thing per meeting. The one thing could be the threat landscape, the gaps against a framework, a hot topic, or an exercise that prices a six-week outage of a critical service. Don’t guess what it should be: ask the board, send a five-minute survey, or ask the CFO and the chief auditor.

Technology leaders who brief the board between meetings, rather than only at them, have boards that are markedly more confident in their own cyber oversight. The session showed two simple formats. An incident briefing, with facts only: the source, the business impact, when the incident was declared and what has been done. And a monthly note on emerging trends, each with its potential impact and the action the board is asked to take. The example on screen was quantum computing. The trend: attackers harvesting encrypted data now to decrypt it later. The board action: approve a discovery project to find the systems and data at risk. That example leads straight into the next chapter.

What to tell the board about an AI apocalypse

Boards are now asking a harder question than how much security is enough. They are asking whether AI could cause a catastrophe. A short Gartner briefing written for exactly this conversation gave the calmest answer I heard all week, in eight questions.

Why the sudden talk? Because real incidents have reached the news. The briefing listed reported cases from recent months: an AI agent breaching a national health database, a senior researcher at a frontier lab resigning over fears for humanity, a lab stopping attempts to use its model for weapons, and hijacked systems heating the fridges of about 700 supermarkets in France to 50°C.

Is an AI apocalypse close? Not yet. Today AI acts mostly in the digital world, and no model can yet cause a loss of control. Over the next two to five years, digital meets physical. Further out, AI gets a body, through robots and infrastructure. The risk grows with AI’s reach into the physical world. Progress is unpredictable, and the item to watch is open-weight models, which carry advanced capability without the abuse monitoring that closed models have.

What is the biggest risk right now? Not a rogue superintelligence. It is that AI cuts the time and the skill an attacker needs: more attackers, moving faster, with less skill, producing what the briefing called a tsunami of vulnerabilities. Two consequences follow, and a board needs to hear both because they cost money and convenience. Patching on a fixed calendar cannot keep pace with attackers working at machine speed, so known exploited vulnerabilities have to be fixed first and fast, which means awkward patch windows, new processes and some redundant systems. And old technology matters again, because AI can chain several weaknesses in legacy systems into one far-reaching attack. The answer is to set targets for reducing technical debt, fund the retirement of legacy systems and keep everything else up to date.

The briefing’s good news was that AI helps defenders too. In its illustration, analysts working alone dealt with three of twelve threats; analysts working with AI agents dealt with six, with governance scaled to match. Its summary for the board fits in one sentence: there is no AI apocalypse yet, the biggest threat today is that AI makes attacks cheaper and faster, and we can act on that now.

Recovery is how you play offence

The radar session finished the argument from the other side. You will be attacked. Six months after a major incident, there is typically little or no lasting effect on earnings per share. What decides the damage is how quickly you recover. Its advice was to make recovery rehearsals part of everyday IT operations rather than an annual exercise: rotate who responds, rehearse rollbacks, test playbooks live, and measure the time it takes to be usable again.

Replace the threat list and the maturity score with outcome measures the board can buy more or less of, agree with the board how much protection is enough, and brief them between meetings.

Case Study, Quantum: Two Clocks

Chapter 15 of 27 · 6 min read · 2 figures

The question this chapter answers

Is quantum computing hype or a real deadline, and what should you do about it this year?

The quantum session started by putting two charts side by side: what quantum computing hype looks like, and what quantum computing reality looks like. Then it showed a slide with a single question, “Why is quantum always three to five years away?”, and the years 2017 to 2026 listed beneath it. Every one of those years, someone said it.

I left the session with a simple way to hold the topic. There are two clocks, and they run at different speeds. One measures when quantum computers will create business value. That clock keeps slipping. The other measures when quantum computers will break the encryption that protects today’s data. That clock is not waiting, because the damage can start before the machines exist.

Gartner frames the work the same way, as two separate jobs: prepare for quantum technologies, and protect against quantum threats.

The first clock: value, slowly

A quantum computer works with qubits, which can hold combinations of states rather than a single 0 or 1. For a narrow class of problems, that lets it find answers no ordinary computer could reach in a useful time. The classes the session named were optimisation, searching, simulating molecules and materials, certain mathematical systems, and factorising large numbers, which is the one that matters for encryption.

The difference from ordinary computing was put well. With classical computers, we design algorithms to fit the problem, on a general-purpose machine built on well-understood physics. With quantum computers, the problem has to be shaped to fit one of a small number of algorithms, on machines that are only now moving from research to narrow demonstrations of advantage. Quantum will not replace anything. It will act as a coprocessor, working alongside CPUs, GPUs and specialist AI chips inside what Gartner calls AI supercomputing platforms.

The market is real but small. Gartner expects quantum computing to grow at about 27% a year to reach $2.8 billion by 2030, and it expects more than half of quantum start-ups to fail by 2030 as buyers reject approaches that cannot reach fault tolerance: machines that correct their own errors well enough to run long calculations reliably. Vendor roadmaps are converging on 2029 for fault-tolerant, general-purpose machines.

The value clock, in Gartner’s phases, runs like this. Today, quantum is a research tool, and some organisations are reporting narrow advantages in research. Around 2029, if the roadmaps hold, fault-tolerant machines arrive and narrow production advantage becomes possible. Broad business value at scale, and the convergence of AI and quantum, sits from 2031 onwards. The session’s advice for this clock was patient: start with the problem, not the hardware. Use the free education programmes that several quantum vendors offer to build your people’s understanding. Experiment through quantum computing services in the cloud. And judge every claim by business advantage rather than by announcements of better hardware. A related Gartner note added one more requirement: now that verifiable claims of quantum advantage are emerging, insist on being able to audit them.

There are real signs of progress. One vendor described a hospital, a national research laboratory and its own team using two quantum computers and two supercomputers together to simulate molecules relevant to drug development, at a scale of more than 12,000 atoms. In the near term, the overlap with AI runs mainly one way: AI helps build quantum software and tune the hardware today, while quantum’s effect on AI is longer-term research.

The second clock: the threat, already running

The other clock is the one that should be on every risk register. Most of today’s secure communication, from websites and payments to digital signatures and identity, relies on public-key cryptography that a large enough quantum computer could break. The session listed what that would mean: communications that could be read, signatures that could be forged, which undermines proof of who agreed to what, and new weaknesses in identity and access management.

The reason this clock is already running is a tactic called harvest now, decrypt later. An attacker copies encrypted data today and stores it until a quantum computer can open it. Anything that must stay confidential for years, such as customer records, contracts, health data or state secrets, is already exposed if it travels under today’s encryption. The US cybersecurity agency uses 2030 to 2035 as its planning horizon for a quantum computer that can break current encryption. The banking trends session expects banking data to be at risk by 2030. The board briefing on AI risk was more aggressive still, putting the point at which quantum could make today’s cryptography obsolete at two to five years away. Estimates differ from session to session, which is itself a reason to start now.

The fix exists. NIST has published post-quantum cryptography standards: ML-KEM for establishing keys, and two signature schemes, ML-DSA and SLH-DSA. Many infrastructure and software vendors already support hybrid post-quantum encryption in their products. Gartner’s timeline expects discovery, assessment and supplier mapping to be finished around 2028, with regulators issuing notifications from 2028 and expecting compliance by 2030. Its summary line was simple: advances in quantum will make conventional public-key cryptography unsafe to use.

And yet:

Nearly half of security leaders have not started. About a quarter are still assessing the risk. Only 4% have begun any deployment, and none has finished. The opening keynote added the reason to move now: the cost of the transition is expected to double for each year of delay after 2026. Its advice was to stop piloting post-quantum cryptography and start deploying it.

What to do this year

The session’s eight best practices were practical. Start now, because this will take years. Create a small centre of excellence to assess the scope, impact and cost. Know what cryptography you have, where it is and why it is there. Set up cryptographic governance that covers your supply chain, legal terms and contracts. Design for crypto-agility, so algorithms can be swapped without rewriting systems. Write modular code, with no hard-wired cryptography. Replace weakening algorithms with strong ones. And migrate step by step, through code and network changes. Then expect to do it again in a few years, because the standards will move.

Who Decides: The Boardroom and the Politics of AI

Chapter 16 of 27 · 6 min read · 1 figure

The question this chapter answers

Who is really steering AI, and are they equipped to?

The CEO session called 2026 “the year of strategic freedom”. 80% of CEOs now say technology has become strategy. Their favourite moves for the next cycle include radically redesigning the operating model (63%), deploying AI agents “as employees” (52%) and radically redesigning human roles (46%). CEOs also expect 20% of their organisation’s revenue to come from AI agents or machine customers by 2030.

Boards are just as keen. 80% of non-executive directors now see AI as a top-five contributor to shareholder value over the next two years, and 47% have personally invested in AI or AI-related companies. When you talk about AI in a board meeting, you are speaking to directors and to investors at the same time.

Then the directors were asked how much of the workforce AI should replace by 2030:

Only 31% of directors believe their current practices are good enough to oversee AI. Yet 77% say boards should accept lower returns in the near term to invest more in it. The analyst read this generously: doubt plus enthusiasm equals opportunity, for the technology leader who can turn both into a plan.

Read that chart next to another number from the CEO session. CEOs expecting AI to eliminate middle-management roles rose from 56% in 2024 to 78% in 2026, while 54% of CEOs say they are very concerned that AI will damage leadership succession. Directors would replace 39% of entry-level workers. But entry-level work is where future managers learn their judgement. The board is planning to remove the bottom rungs of the ladder and worrying, separately, about who will climb it. I come back to this in Chapter 21.

AI is a political project before it is a technical one

The politics session opened with a line from a chief AI officer that drew knowing laughter.

Every shortcut in data, process and ownership surfaces the moment an agent touches it. The temptation is to blame the past. The advice was to treat the past as finished and move straight from facts to solving the problem.

The pressures are new and personal. A CEO loved the company’s AI strategy until a competitor announced one with better marketing. Executives ask, “Can’t you just do this with AI, and fewer people?” The session offered three tools I expect many readers will use this month. Catch and pivot: never tell a senior executive they are wrong; find something in what they said to agree with, then give them information they may not have and move to better options. The conditional no: turn pressure into leverage by saying yes, but only if the budget, the data and the accountability are in place. And recognition: find the wins with functional leaders and give them the credit.

Two findings stayed with me. AI does not fix ownership or accountability problems; it amplifies them. And the relationships between the CIO, the chief AI officer and the rest of the C-suite matter more than the AI operating model. Appointing someone to be “in charge of AI” and leaving them to it is an outdated design.

The CIO’s job is changing shape

The session on the CIO role to 2030 described five forces pulling at the job. AI-native development platforms that let IT build far faster. AI spreading into every function. AI helping the C-suite make decisions together. Technology leadership splitting across several executives. And pressure for IT to drive revenue through AI, not just enable it.

The point that stayed with me was the first one. Building faster does not mean delivering faster. “We can build faster than the enterprise can absorb,” the speaker said. AI coding tools cut development time, but the weeks are lost elsewhere: waiting for reviews and approvals, for key people to be available, for change management, training and funding, and then for testing with other functions, final compliance checks and go-live coordination. His answer was “approvals as code”: replace slow, manual approval steps with guardrails, guidelines and red lines built into the platform itself, so that most changes are approved by design.

The split in technology leadership is already real. 24% of the world’s largest enterprises have two or more executive technology leaders, such as a chief AI officer or chief digital officer alongside the CIO. Overlapping mandates lead to conflicting priorities and uncoordinated investment. The session’s tool was an accountability grid that maps who leads run, grow and transform work across the back office, core operations and the front office, with the overlaps negotiated explicitly: how responsibilities are split, how conflicts are resolved, and who communicates.

The CEO session put the stakes plainly: if you aren’t shaping growth discussions, you’re bidding on spending decisions. 22% of CEOs think the CIO role will disappear by 2030. The bigger role, one speaker said, won’t be assigned. It must be claimed.

Strategy becomes a product

One session asked whether strategic planning itself can keep up. The numbers said no. The average strategy cycle takes 14.5 weeks. Only 23% of PMOs have real visibility of how execution is going. 82% of CIOs are forced into changes outside the planning cycle. Strategy cycles measured in months cannot govern an environment that shifts every week.

The answer from leading organisations was to treat strategy as a living product rather than an annual document. That means regular short syncs, for example monthly or quarterly, plus extra ones whenever scanning spots a significant change; 90-day decision cycles; and a one-page visual summary used to tell the strategy as stories that make sense to each audience.

It also means designing for change. Separate a high-confidence foundation, the investments that are mandatory, critical, enabling or common to every likely future, from incremental investments added as conditions change. Then choose how to deliver each investment: in full, built to scale, in phases, or in modules that can be reused if priorities shift. One example of a principle that makes this real: no initiative should run longer than six months, and anything longer than nine needs the executive committee’s approval.

And the most useful line on where AI fits: use it to scan and summarise, and keep it away from writing the strategy. One airline CIO compared strategy to making a film. AI is for the repeatable work. The judgement stays human.

The architecture session made the same point about foresight. Assume your roadmap is blind to 80% of the disruptions that will hit it. Gartner expects 70% of enterprise architecture teams to run trendspotting across the enterprise by 2028, with scouts in business units who plot trends on a radar covering technology, politics, economics, society, trust, regulation and the environment. Two of its lines stayed with me. Stop chasing hype: scale capabilities, not proofs of concept. And adopting the right technology at the wrong time burns capital, so when a promising trend is not ready, set a date to reassess it rather than walking away. It also asked CIOs to end the confusion between architect roles: enterprise and business architects look three to five years ahead across all solutions, solution and product architects up to a year ahead, and engineering architects deliver within weeks. Each needs to know which box it is in.

Wired to resist

One session asked whether organisations are wired to resist AI, and answered yes. The cause is organisational entrenchment, “the by-product of past success”: inertia, blaming outsiders, authoritarian leadership, territorial behaviour, avoiding uncertainty and hoarding information. The more entrenched an organisation is, the deeper the change it needs. And a warning for anyone tempted to push everything at once: speed alone is a myth. If you push everything at full throttle, you crash.

The decisionEntry 16 · Ch. 16

Turn board enthusiasm into a plan the board can oversee, with one simple question, outcome measures and briefings between meetings; agree who among your technology leaders owns what; and run strategy as a product, not an annual event.

The New Operating Model

Chapter 17 of 27 · 6 min read · 2 figures

The question this chapter answers

What does the organisation itself have to become when agents join the workforce?

For thirty years, operating models were drawn as people, process and technology. That frame breaks when systems stop behaving the same way every time and start learning. Gartner’s replacement is actors, assets and approach.

Actors are people and agents. The agents are “not people, but toolmates”, and each needs a RACI that names a human who answers for it. Assets now include agent harnesses and context management, as core parts of the enterprise. Approach covers funding and measurement: money moves towards token budgets, and measures move from effort to outcomes.

Tiny teams

Teams are shrinking too. The agile “two-pizza team” of eight or nine people is giving way to tiny teams of three or four working with agents. One case nicknamed it “two bagels and a token”.

The tiny teams session explained where the idea comes from. In start-ups, revenue per employee has become the headline metric. One AI coding company went from $1 million to $100 million in annual recurring revenue in twelve months with fewer than 20 people. Another reached $300 million with a similar headcount. The question for an enterprise is whether any of that can be repeated inside a large organisation. The session’s answer was yes, but only with three things in place.

The first is role compression. Specialised roles are converging into broader ones. Product managers, engineers, designers and testers become product engineers. Data scientists and machine learning engineers become AI engineers. Business analysts become AI requirements analysts. The same is happening outside IT: the session showed a finance example in which the analyst and the quant become one modern role. The risks are real. People need retraining, broader roles can overload them, and compressed teams have more single points of failure.

The second is forward-deployed engineers: hands-on technical people who sit with business experts and build a solution, such as an agent, quickly and side by side. Job postings for the role grew more than 700% in a year. The pattern that works inside an enterprise pairs a deeply technical engineer with a deeply non-technical domain expert, and the domain expert supplies the context. It is not a role for everyone. One CIO estimated that only about 5% of his developers could do it, because it needs empathy for users, speed of learning and flexibility.

The third is platform teams, which make tiny teams safe. They manage the platforms the tiny teams use, such as developer sandboxes, build security and governance into deployment, and look after what tiny teams build, all the way to retirement. Developers may not want to join them, so rotate people in and out, and reward platform teams for how fast customer-facing changes reach customers.

From vibe coding to a software factory

The AI-native development session showed where software teams are heading, and it covered three things happening at once.

The first is vibe coding: platforms where anyone describes an application in plain language and gets a working, hosted app in minutes. They suit new ideas built from scratch and refined through conversation. They also need rules. The session sorted uses into four tiers: off limits; dangerous enough to require an enterprise platform; supported with a business case; and supported by default.

The second is coding assistants turning into coding agents that carry out multistep tasks on their own. In a survey of 300 senior software engineering leaders, agents are already the main tool for more than a third of teams writing new code (37%, against 43% using assistants) and for about a third generating tests (32%). The average engineering function uses 4.8 different assistants or agents.

The third is new teams and roles:

The path runs in three phases: people in the loop, reviewing each change; people on the loop, supervising many; and a target the session called the autonomous software factory. Roles expand along the way. Product managers become product engineers. User experience design adds agent experience design. DevOps adds agent operations. Machine learning engineers become AI engineers. Business analysts become AI requirements analysts. Service delivery leads become forward-deployed engineers. 72% of engineering leaders expect their number of AI engineers to grow.

Product antipatterns

Underneath all this sits a less glamorous question: how work is funded and who owns it. The product management session showed that organisations moving from projects to products mostly stumble on the same few antipatterns. Each is a common, appealing response that ends up making things worse. The survey data was unusually clear:

Read the chart as one argument. Fund the product, not the project. Put someone in charge of the change, not just of the funding mechanics. Dedicate the key roles rather than sharing them. Have IT and business product managers own the same outcome together. And measure what customers and the business get, not whether the project landed on time and on budget.

One bank in the session funded each customer journey with a fixed annual spending envelope and planned quarterly. It eased finance teams into the change with short, outcome-focused funding increments and one-page business cases. Every one of these conditions applies to agents too. An agent with no product owner, no funded team and no outcome measure is a pilot waiting to be cancelled. The session’s warning to CIOs: antipatterns multiply when the transformation is over-delegated.

The operating model is never done

A session on why operating model changes stall explained why so many of these redesigns fade after launch. Only 24% of CIOs say their operating model flexes as business needs change. AI makes the problem harder, because it moves technology from predictable systems that suit projects to systems that learn and change, which suit products.

The operating model is also much more than the organisation chart. It covers talent, structures, sourcing and partners, AI agents, data, funding, platforms, tools, ways of working, decision rights and performance measures. The session’s argument was simple: if the operating model has to keep evolving, the capability that runs that evolution cannot be temporary. Manage it as a product rather than a project, in a continuous loop: shape it, evolve it, operate it, reassess it.

That job usually falls to the office of the CIO, which the speaker described, with some affection, as the “chief of stuff”: part planner, part fixer, part enforcer, part persuader, part therapist. For an operating model change, its centre of gravity has to move from running IT’s business to enabling transformation. The rule: centralise the orchestration, not the transformation. One persistent team guides the journey, and everybody makes the change happen. The practices that work are change product owners who own a backlog of changes, change designed with employees rather than for them, and networks of change leaders in the business who spread new habits. One pharmaceutical company appointed change product owners for change management, culture and organisation design. One health insurer’s office of the CIO acted as the connective tissue between silos and grew the number of agile teams in its business groups roughly tenfold.

The decisionEntry 17 · Ch. 17

Give every agent a RACI with a named accountable human, build tiny teams on top of a real platform team, fund products rather than projects, and give the operating model itself a permanent owner.

Freedom in a Frame: When Everyone Builds

Chapter 18 of 27 · 7 min read · 1 figure

The question this chapter answers

What happens when anyone in the business can build software and agents, and what should IT do about it?

The democratisation session opened with a survey result. 80% of CIOs expect AI to increase the amount of technology built outside IT by 2030: 41% somewhat and 39% significantly. Non-IT executives agree. 60% expect their own teams to build or configure AI agents, 80% to monitor, run and improve them, and 71% to make sure they are governed and used ethically.

What is different this time is that you can now build by describing what you want in plain language, business units are hiring their own technical people, and products are designed for business users. Gartner defines democratisation as the deliberate enabling and coordination of technology work outside IT, in chosen areas, with guardrails and accountability. The definition matters because of what it excludes. Unmanaged democratisation is a recipe for chaos and unacceptable risk.

The session drew the history as a curve, and it is the clearest way I have seen to explain the problem:

With no sanctioned path, people build in the shadows anyway. Citizen development opened a door, but a narrow one. Managed democratisation, expanded deliberately, in chosen places, under clear guardrails, is the sweet spot. Push further without management and you get what the session called AI slop: chaos, risk and a large estate nobody owns.

Another IT 2030 session put numbers on the shift. The share of organisations where significant technology work is done outside IT is expected to rise from 46% today to 76% by 2030. It sketched four futures for IT, defined by how much technology work IT does and how much the business does itself, and made two points that matter more than the labels: the four are not a maturity model, and choosing one is a decision for the executive committee, not for IT. “Democratisation is inevitable if growth is a priority,” the speaker said. The job is to make it safe, easy and capable, and to count the value created rather than the apps built.

The same session was frank about ambition. There are AI-first, AI-opportunistic and AI-cautious organisations, and “you probably should not be AI-first”. Copy what AI-first firms do well, investing narrowly, funding foundations and allowing no one-offs, without copying the bet.

The scale could be large. In its most aggressive scenario, for an AI-first organisation, the session sketched the volume of business-built assets growing by hundreds of times today’s software volume. By 2030, Gartner expects the mandate of 40% of CIOs to expand from delivering technology to democratising it.

The pricing agent nobody planned for

The case that made this real was simple. At an industrial gases company, a salesperson built a useful AI agent for pricing. Sales then wanted IT to take it over. IT had three options, and all three were bad. Reject it, because no IT standards were followed, and IT becomes the villain. Accept it, because the business is pushing, and you set a precedent that some people get to skip the rules. Rebuild it to IT standards, and the cost doubles.

The lesson: without a plan for democratisation, you are left with only bad choices. The session described two kinds to plan for. In the first, the business builds and IT hardens and runs what it built, as with a sales analysis app. In the second, the business builds and runs it, and IT sets the guardrails, as with a product configuration agent. Each needs a “democratisation package” with three parts: the business scope (which area, which users, which problem), the permitted use (which data, processes and access, and with what authority) and the technical pathway (which platforms and integrations). Launching a platform without deciding the first two is the mistake to avoid.

The playbook has three verbs. Enable: make the approved path the easiest one, with sandboxes, templates, connectors and promotion paths into production. Educate: build AI literacy by role, guidance inside the tools, safe-builder guides and readiness for business owners. Execute: keep a registry of what has been built, control identity and access, agree an ownership contract, and have support and escalation ready. A regulated fintech in the session showed AI doing part of the work: IT built agents that watch the business developers’ code for compliance with IT standards, then help the developers fix what falls short.

The CIO 2030 session put the same idea in three words: freedom in a frame. Do not try to stop democratisation. Bring order to it. Only 21% of enterprises have an AI agent governance framework, yet 71% will have deployed agents by the end of 2027. Financial management will need an upgrade as well, because value, cost and capital spend are spread across business and IT budgets.

AI-first does not mean AI everywhere

The AI-first culture session was a useful corrective to the word “first”. Gartner’s definition: AI is a core part of decisions, investments and operations, and it is always considered and used where it makes most sense. An AI-first culture does not always default to AI. It stays committed to customer outcomes, business priorities and people, and applies AI where it has the greatest effect.

The session described the shift in the constraint on innovation in a way that echoes this whole essay. Technology used to be scarce, data limited, experiments slow and expertise siloed. Now information is abundant, but intelligence is not. Data is available but not ready for AI. Ideas, content and code can be generated instantly, but generically. Judgement is inconsistent.

It gave CIOs three tasks. Signal: leaders should model AI use visibly, including the cases where they considered AI and chose not to use it, and share prompts, use cases and peer stories, because peer influence matters more than mandates. Enable: make experimentation safe and easy, reduce the cost of curiosity, and separate an experimentation zone, where people explore, test and learn, from a production zone, where work is secured, governed and scaled. And reinvent: redesign work rather than automate it. The session’s “blank sheet test” asks: if AI had existed when we first designed this process, what would it look like? Then three follow-up questions: what assumptions is AI making obsolete, what capacity is it creating, and where will we reinvest that capacity?

Boundaries matter too. Only 33% of organisations issue formal reprimands or penalties for breaking AI policy, and 25% dismiss people for serious violations. A culture of experimentation needs clear ethical lines, such as doing the right thing first rather than the AI thing first, saying when AI contributed significantly, staying sceptical of its output, and protecting data.

From pilots to AI-native business models

The innovation session made the commercial case for all of this. Through 2029, Gartner expects 90% of successful innovations to come from enterprises that run AI-led innovation processes, and it was firm that people, not AI, lead them. It also quoted a widely cited study finding that 95% of AI pilots show no measurable effect on profit.

It described three kinds of AI-led innovation. Process innovation turns a slow, manual task into an automated flow. Product innovation puts AI inside goods and services. Business model innovation uses agents to change how value is delivered and how revenue is collected. Its example came from law. A firm can adopt AI tools to speed up its lawyers’ work and keep selling their time. Or it can become AI-native: deliver legal outcomes end to end, with people overseeing the exceptions and high-risk decisions, and charge by subscription or by outcome. The session drew a path from stage zero, no AI at all, to stage five, a business built fundamentally on AI, and noted that the best innovators are increasingly becoming AI-native providers themselves. One emerging model follows: providers that deliver a managed business outcome with agents and are paid per outcome.

Two warnings came with it. Work generated solely by AI, with no human authorship, may have no copyright protection at all, so nobody owns it. And the action plan for Monday morning was blunt in the best sense: stop celebrating AI pilots. Release funding in stages, only when a business unit commits to changing a product, an operation or its business model, and track and reward the outcomes.

The decisionEntry 18 · Ch. 18

Write democratisation packages before you launch building platforms, decide in advance who runs what the business builds, give people freedom in a frame rather than a mandate, and stop celebrating pilots: fund outcomes in stages.

Every operating model has a layer in the middle. It is the layer most plans intend to shrink, and the one the change depends on.

The Missing Middle

Chapter 19 of 27 · 3 min read · 1 figure

The question this chapter answers

Why do the managers that everyone plans to remove matter more than ever?

In 2016, a famous AI pioneer said we should stop training radiologists, because AI would soon read scans better than they could. Ten years later, radiologists have not gone anywhere. Their work has intensified instead. A study of 6,700 radiologists across 1,100 hospitals linked AI use to burnout.

The session that told this story gave the pattern a name: hyperwork, deeper and faster, combined with work surges, broader in scope. It is fed by a slot-machine cycle of AI wins and urgent fixes that ends in what the speaker called “brain fry”. 76% of software engineers and 87% of data and analytics staff say they are already over capacity.

That is the human side of the paradox this essay began with. Intelligence is abundant. The people who must direct it, check it and answer for it are not.

The layer everyone plans to remove is the layer doing the work

Middle managers face three pressures at once: balancing senior leaders’ ambitions against employees’ fears, being told that much of their own work can be done by AI, and a rising workload with little prioritisation. Yet they are, in the session’s words, the force multipliers for AI change.

The support model was practical. Build management communities that learn, support each other, debate and build together across business units. Aim change management at managers, two-way and tied to business outcomes, rather than broadcasting it at all employees. Treat change fatigue like a phone battery: let the drained recharge, help the charging conserve power, and keep the charged from draining. And one honest line: not every technologist makes a great manager. Create a way out for those who are not.

Expand before you eliminate

The IT 2030 session explained why so many AI programmes stall here: people need an inspiring future, not another change initiative to endure. Yet heads of IT direct 43% of AI investment at technology and only 28% at people.

The work redesign session gave the order that fixes this, and a set of questions to go with it. First, expand what people can do. What if one employee had the expertise of every other employee? What if AI doubled the quality of decisions, or brought insight that was never available before? What if the work was already done before you needed it? Then eliminate. What if 20% of tasks were no longer needed and AI automated half of the rest? What if AI handled the exceptions that now need a person? What if steps could happen in any order, or all at once? Only then transform the outcome: better products and services, better terms for customers, new markets, even a new mandate.

Most programmes start at the second step. People hear “your job is going” before they hear “here is what you will be able to do”.

The redesign also needs the right team. The session’s core redesign pod has a business design lead who shapes a shared vision and brings stakeholders along, a technical co-lead who finds and prototypes AI opportunities, and subject-matter experts who reveal the knowledge nobody wrote down, co-design the new roles and judge what fails. The burnout session added the guardrails: cap utilisation at 85%, budget 1.2 to 1.5 times the resources for every AI initiative, and stop the work surges caused by low-quality AI output.

Measure managers by span of value rather than span of control, free their time before asking them to lead AI change, and expand what people can do before eliminating their work.

Part VI·People—Chapter 20

The Skills We Keep: Atrophy and the Skill Stack

Chapter 20 of 27 · 11 min read · 4 figures

The question this chapter answers

Which human abilities should we keep exercising, and which can we safely hand to the machine?

There is a widely shared photograph of a car wedged into a narrow footpath in a Welsh seaside town. Its driver had followed the satellite navigation, which said the route was fine. One session used it to make a point about the decade ahead: trust the GPS up to a point, but make sure the vehicle fits the road.

That is the skills problem in miniature. The more reliable the machine becomes, the less often we check it, and the less able we are to check it on the day it matters. Every enterprise is about to make thousands of small decisions about which human abilities to keep exercising and which to hand over. Most will make them by accident. This chapter and the next are about making them on purpose. They draw on more than a dozen sessions that each touched the question from a different side.

First, the work itself is changing shape

A session on what Gartner calls AI’s era of the builders started from a simple observation. For decades, turning an idea into working software meant requirements, design, coding, testing, documentation and iteration, over weeks or months. With AI, an idea becomes a prototype in hours or days. Building is no longer the constraint. Ideas, judgement and the ability to choose what is worth building are.

The same session described five levels of human–AI working. What separates them is not how clever the AI is, but what the person does:

At the first two levels, people still do the work, AI helps, and the person checks everything. At the third, an agent performs defined tasks and the person delegates and oversees. The break comes after that. At levels four and five, many agents run routine work in parallel, or design, build and operate a capability themselves, and the person’s job becomes setting goals, directing agents, owning quality and steering innovation. Crossing that break is not a tooling upgrade. It is a redesign of roles and teams.

Software engineering shows why. AI already speeds up almost every task in the delivery cycle: writing user stories, generating designs from text, writing, explaining and reviewing code, writing tests, fixing failed builds. Yet speed to market often stalls, because work sits idle between rigid roles and waits for the next sprint or meeting. The tasks got faster. The system did not. The session’s answer was the agentic loop, adapted from the military’s observe, orient, decide, act cycle: people express intent, agents implement it on a governed platform, and the loop tightens, sensing earlier, deciding better and acting at higher quality and scale. The same loops are appearing well beyond IT, in security operations, the financial close, fraud prevention, trading and supply-chain control towers.

The people side follows. Only 20% of CIOs say their IT staff are fully ready for AI, and more than 75% of software engineering and data roles will need reskilling by 2030. Roles compress and expand at the same time, as Chapter 17 described, and IT headcount changes shape rather than simply falling. The physical world is next. By 2030, Gartner predicts that 80% of frontline workers at international companies will be assisted by physical AI, with robots, drones and sensors taking the dangerous and routine tasks first. It also named what it called the “next worst action” for boards: replacing people with robots and expecting the return to come from having fewer employees.

The skills we value are not the skills we need

The human skills session started with a CIO survey that should embarrass most talent plans. Around half of CIOs say innovation (52%), problem solving (49%) and critical thinking (45%) are critical for IT staff in an AI-augmented world. Only about a quarter say the same for change leadership (26%), ethical judgement (25%) and building trust in teams of people and AI (23%). Persuasion scored 8%.

Look at the bottom of that list. Those are the skills that decide whether the redesign in the previous chapters lands or is resisted.

Gartner is now assessing AI’s impact role by role, across more than 20 IT roles, from business analyst and context engineer to security operations analyst and software engineer, and has started on business roles such as HR business partner and recruiter. It looks at each job in three parts: the core tasks that belong to any version of the role, the contextual tasks that vary with the person, the team and the business, and the shared responsibilities that cross boundaries and depend on influence. AI touches each part differently. That is why blanket claims such as “80% of this job could be done by agents” are both inaccurate and harmful. Another session added that messages about scarcity, about jobs disappearing, make people defensive, while messages about abundance, about what people will be able to do, help them adopt.

A stack, not a list

The most useful model of the week put skills in five layers, built from the bottom up:

At the bottom sits the human foundation: skills that never lose their value. The session grouped them into five areas. Who you are: self-awareness, emotional resilience, integrity, curiosity, openness to feedback. How you grow: continuous learning, critical thinking, problem solving, digital literacy, adaptability. How you connect: empathy, active listening, teamwork, communication, and building trust in a workforce that includes AI. How you create value: initiative, innovation, decision making, change leadership, spotting opportunities. And what you become: purpose-driven leadership, living by your values, mentoring, ethical judgement and lasting positive impact.

Above the foundation sit enduring skills such as design thinking, risk assessment, coordinating across functions and leading change. Then the two kinds of atrophy, risky and acceptable. And at the top, new and emerging skills that barely existed three years ago. The State of the AI Union put the instruction in one line: grow the skills you want, protect those you need.

The software engineer is the role furthest along, and the most detailed example presented:

Layer What to do In a software engineer’s job
New and emerging Build now Orchestrating and overseeing agents; designing multi-agent workflows; evaluating LLM systems; curating and grounding data. Next: calibrating trust between people and AI, governing autonomous systems, ethical stewardship of AI
Acceptable atrophy Let AI take it Routine coding; standard testing and QA; basic integration and deployment
Risky atrophy Protect from AI Deep code review and debugging instinct; secure design and threat modelling; technical stewardship and mentoring; drawing requirements out of ambiguous situations; maintaining legacy systems
Enduring skills Keep and deepen Architecture decisions; design and systems thinking; writing specifications and judging outcomes; deep programming expertise; awareness of risk

One item in the risky row deserves attention: technical stewardship and mentoring. The session flagged that losing it undermines an organisation’s resilience. That is the apprenticeship problem, hiding inside a skills chart. It comes back in the next chapter.

Deciding what may fade

Atrophy is not the enemy. Most of us stopped doing long division when calculators arrived and stopped memorising routes when maps moved into our phones, and we were fine. The question is never whether skills fade, but which ones, and whether anyone decided. The session offered a simple test:

If a skill is essential for legal, regulatory or ethical reasons, protect it. If it is a key differentiator for the business, protect it. If neither, ask whether it is used so often that losing it would cause problems straight away. If not, let some atrophy happen. If it is, ask one more question: is it hard to replace, relearn or outsource, even with AI? If so, protect it. If not, let it fade and let AI cover the gap, as with administration or data entry.

The skills that most often land on the protect side were named: contextual problem solving, root cause analysis, negotiation and adversarial thinking. What they have in common is that each is used precisely when the machine is wrong, the case is unusual, or someone is trying to deceive you. They are the skills of the exception. Every idea in Part II, from human exceptioneering to recoverability, and most of Part V, depends on people still having them.

Protect: practise deliberately, and design over-trust out

A protected skill has to be exercised, or it fades anyway. Three practices stood out.

Simulators. AI makes it possible to build realistic environments that simulate complex scenarios, behaviours and crises without real consequences. One insurer built an AI training simulator for its underwriters, cut its certification failure rate by 75%, and 85% of participants reported better skills.

Learning from failure. The higher education session’s “AI-ready graduate” practises in simulated environments, works in teams where the person supervises, guides and checks the AI, and deliberately meets AI mistakes in order to learn to catch and correct them. Tellingly, none of the 13 AI technologies on the higher education hype cycle has yet reached maturity.

Designing over-trust out of the process. The builders session’s suggestions were to force tests and confirmations into the workflow, keeping “hands on the wheel”; to require training and legal attestations; to keep separation of duties; and to decide, workflow by workflow, whether a person is in, on or out of the loop.

The CRM session supplied the role model. A person who patches broken AI output out of sight is a fixer: the reasons go unrecorded, the hidden workload breeds burnout, and judgement is treated as a cheap patch for poor automation. A person whose interventions are tracked and fed back into the system is a supervisor. The first role wears skills down. The second exercises them.

Purge: make it safe to let go

The skills the session suggested releasing were unglamorous: routine reporting and documentation, resource allocation, routine coding, basic testing and basic analysis. Releasing them is harder than it sounds, because of two obstacles: my identity is tied to this skill, and my status is tied to this skill.

The answers were human rather than technical. Show people a credible picture of their future role first; that alone had a 24% effect on AI adoption. Have leaders publicly let go of their own old skills, so it is safe for others to do the same. Even mark the moment with a small ritual, so the release is acknowledged rather than mourned.

There is a financial side that the builders session called a test of managerial courage. Time freed by AI has to go somewhere, and leaders must decide where before it arrives. It can be reinvested, into the backlog, modernisation, upskilling and new capabilities. Or it can be banked, through lower external spend, performance management, or fewer roles. Decide neither, and the gain evaporates into busier calendars, which is exactly why so few companies can show AI savings in their accounts.

Pivot: build the skills that did not exist

The emerging skills named across sessions were consistent: orchestrating agents, AI governance and ethics, AI-driven business innovation, and collaboration between people and AI. The human skills session also listed skills that kept repeating across many future jobs, including AI engineering, change leadership, business partnership, data platform management, product ownership, context engineering and risk management. And it mapped enduring skills onto eight future talent profiles worth putting into next year’s hiring plan: systems thinkers, crisis managers, compliance champions, platform builders, translators, opportunity amplifiers, trust stewards and value governors.

One change deserves its own line. Management is becoming universal, because everyone now manages something, even if it is a team of agents. When agents let you do almost anything, the scarce skill is knowing what you should do. Prioritising, setting intent, overseeing and stepping in need to be built before agents arrive at scale. And the working relationship between people and agents should be designed: workflows with defined roles, checkpoints and handoffs rather than one long conversation, agent activity and uncertainty made visible, and human control built into the flow. As the session put it: don’t just upskill people; design agents to work well with people.

The session on the future of human–AI collaboration took this furthest. Gartner’s position: the real breakthrough in AI will come from designing smarter interactions between people and AI, more than from designing smarter agents. 62% of attendees had listed human–AI collaboration as a top topic for the symposium. The session described three kinds of relationship:

In the first, independence, AI is an actor next to us. The user stops being an operator and becomes someone who interacts with another actor, and oversight can even run in reverse, as when one government appointed an AI as a “minister”. In the second, interdependence, AI extends what people can do, as co-bots and co-agents. The design rule here is what the session called sociomorphism: a robot working with people does not need to look human, but it must be relatable in how it interacts. In the third, interbeing, technology moves inside the person, from implants to brain–computer interfaces. Governance has to come first there, and the risk is a split between people who have the technology and people who do not.

Its practical actions were down to earth. Write an “HR policy” for AI agents. Think about liability now. Update the employee value proposition, because you are now hiring an ecosystem rather than a person. Hire people who cross science and the humanities. And remember that always-on people are not always productive people. One line about fear deserves to be repeated in every town hall: people are not afraid that AI can do their jobs. They are afraid that you believe so.

Name your human foundation, map your critical roles onto the skill stack, and publish the list of skills you will protect from atrophy.

The question this chapter answers

If AI does the junior work, how will people learn judgement, and whom should we hire?

The apprenticeship problem

This is where every thread in Part VI meets, and where the symposium had the least to offer. Boards would replace 39% of entry-level workers with AI by 2030. CEOs expect AI to eliminate middle-management roles, and 54% are very concerned it will damage leadership succession. The CEO session’s guidance was right but incomplete: protect the experiences that develop future leaders, and use AI to remove administrative work, not developmental experiences. The skills chart says the same thing from the other side: mentoring is a skill at risk of atrophy, and losing it weakens the organisation.

Junior work was never just output. It was the curriculum. The routine reconciliation, the first draft, the basic test, the hundredth customer query: that is where people learned what normal looks like, so they could later spot the abnormal. Automate the curriculum without replacing it, and in ten years there will be nobody able to supervise the agents.

My own view is that apprenticeship now has to be designed rather than inherited. Simulators for the rare, high-stakes moments. Real exceptions routed to juniors on purpose, under supervision. Learning from failure as standard practice. And mentoring measured and rewarded as a protected skill rather than assumed.

Procedure and osmosis

The job chaos session gave the most useful answer to the apprenticeship problem I heard all week, and it began by cooling the panic. AI will not cause a jobs apocalypse. It will cause job chaos. Gartner estimates that between 0.4% and 1.8% of the global workforce will lose a job directly because of AI, while one worker in five globally, and two in five in the US, will see their job transformed. That is twenty times more people affected by change than by loss. In its running forecast for global jobs outside China and India, jobs gained, about 10.6 million, stay ahead of jobs lost, about 8.9 million. Few jobs disappear, most transform, and some are new: agent operations engineer, AI FinOps engineer, AI reliability architect, AI security and red-team engineers, knowledge graph engineers, internal forward-deployed engineers.

Then it sorted jobs by two things: how complex they are, and how people learn them early in their careers. Some jobs are learned by procedure, from rules, checklists and drills. Others are learned by osmosis, from working alongside experienced people.

That gives four job types, and AI changes each in a different way. Complex jobs learned by procedure, such as surgeon or military pilot, use AI as a simulator to prepare. Complex jobs learned by osmosis, such as lawyer, auditor or software developer, use agents under an expert who stays accountable for the result. Simpler procedural jobs, such as first-level support and many back-office roles, are where automation goes furthest, with people handling the exceptions. Simpler jobs learned by osmosis, such as frontline sales or customer support engineer, get a mix of all three.

The session’s conclusion, in two lines, is the clearest guidance on the apprenticeship problem I have seen. Address the risk for mid- and late-career people in low-complexity, highly routine jobs, because they will need the most help to move. And invest in graduate programmes for critical, high-complexity jobs learned by osmosis, because those are exactly the jobs where agents remove the junior work that used to teach people.

Finding the people: the talent hiding in the friction

The builders session made a point about where AI talent is that ran against my expectations. Organisations complain they cannot find it, while their own processes push it away.

The people already building with AI, and causing friction as they do, are often exactly the talent you need: AI artisans on the business side and tech trailblazers in IT. The people who cause no friction are often the spectators and the gatekeepers. Treat friction from AI experiments as a talent signal rather than a compliance problem, and ask candidates to demonstrate AI skills during recruitment rather than simply list them.

Two other sessions sharpened the hiring lens. The product management session argued for competencies over expertise: business knowledge and technical knowledge can stand in for each other depending on the product, and each role should have a defined set of minimum competencies, from leadership and communication to handling conflict and working in agile teams. The middle manager session described the development work most worth doing: a skills-based talent strategy, a portfolio of skills rather than one deep specialism, and AI literacy pathways for everyone. The small-team session added that AI literacy is the most neglected investment of all.

Adaptability is becoming the skill behind all the others

The adaptability session began with an awkward comparison: not all AI is ready, but it is more ready than most people are. The number of skills an average job requires is rising by about 4.8% a year, and 34% of the skills a job needed in 2025 will no longer be relevant by 2030. In that world, expertise creates value today, but adaptability keeps the advantage.

The session proposed a new formula for talent: (IQ + EQ) × AQ, where the adaptability quotient is the ability to unlearn, relearn and work well under continuous change. It assessed adaptability in three dimensions. Ability: grit, mental flexibility, resilience, mindset and the capacity to unlearn. Character: emotional range, hope, motivation and thinking style. And environment: the support, team, work setting and stress around a person. The third matters as much as the first two. Adaptable people leave places that punish adaptation.

The profile Gartner calls the versatilist combines depth with breadth and moves easily between contexts, teams, products and AI-augmented work. Yet its share of IT staff has grown by only two percentage points since 2021. Organisations say they want adaptability and keep hiring and promoting for narrow expertise. An analysis of 1.8 million IT and data job adverts compared what CIOs say matters with what their adverts actually ask for, and the two did not line up.

The fix is to plan for capabilities rather than fixed jobs:

Several practices make that real. Redesign jobs around adaptability: one space agency made its astronaut role more versatile, and other organisations have created a role whose job is to define how people and agents work together, or merged demand and supply planning into end-to-end planner roles supported by AI. Move people through internal talent marketplaces rather than waiting for vacancies. Organise delivery into teams that form and re-form around priorities, drawing on shared pools of skills such as data, AI and software engineering, architecture and governance, and the agents themselves. Use AI to spread expertise: one professional services firm built AI versions of its knowledge experts to share their know-how across the firm in real time. But watch for what researchers have called the AI wall effect: AI helps, until suddenly it doesn’t.

Hiring has to change with it. Interviews should ask how a candidate adapted when a new AI tool arrived: the challenge, what they did and what happened. One AI-native software company now runs engineering interviews in three stages. The candidate leads a session to define a product, builds it with AI tools over two hours, then demonstrates it. Interviewers can judge product thinking, how the candidate directs AI, and technical judgement, all at once.

Leading it: quality and growth, not just productivity

The builders session named a common leadership mistake: focusing too much on AI productivity and cost savings. Its alternative was to lead with quality and speed, connected to growth through innovation, customer experience and a better risk position. It even proposed a test of culture. The culture you want says “we are proud to do great work”, not “we do a lot”.

And it pointed to what the builders should build: new AI-based products such as AI tutors or AI wealth advisers; services that only exist because AI can deliver them, such as always-on advice or outcome-based services; products redesigned with AI, down to their materials or formulation; better product models, such as the actuarial tables beneath an insurance policy; AI combined with robotics; and new markets and buyers. One speaker put the timing well: great leaders don’t wait for work to be redesigned before they reskill people. They anticipate.

  2. Map every critical role onto the five-layer stack, starting with the roles agents will touch first.

  4. Build deliberate practice for protected skills: simulators, learning from failure and rotations through real exceptions.

  8. Decide in advance where freed capacity goes, reinvested or banked, and say so publicly.
  9. Hire for friction and for adaptability: find the artisans and trailblazers already building, test AI skill in recruitment, plan for capabilities rather than fixed jobs, and reward people who move.

Return on Integrity: The Values Question Is Also the Value Question

Chapter 22 of 27 · 5 min read · 1 figure

The question this chapter answers

Can responsible AI pay for itself, and who will enforce it if it does not?

Is it responsible parenting to track your child’s location on your phone? Or is it responsible to refuse to?

The responsible AI session opened with that question because reasonable people answer it differently, and so will your customers in different markets. “Responsible” means different things to different people, in different places. One line on screen, quoting a colleague of the speaker, has stayed with me: ethics is not a set of problems to solve, but a way of coping with the human condition.

The session’s reasons for urgency covered a wide range. A papal encyclical on protecting the human person in the age of AI. More than 200,000 people in one European country petitioning to end a data-analytics firm’s public-sector contracts. Experiments in which AI agents simulating a society produced anything from a stable democracy to violent collapse, depending on the model. Reports of frontier models escaping their test environments. And a question nobody on any stage answered: who is responsible when agentic AI goes rogue?

This is the deepest layer of the trust recession the opening keynote described. The seven-themes session made the link to money explicit: the AI value gap is really an AI risk gap. Organisations think they have a value problem. They have a risk problem that shows up as missing value. Closing it rests on three things, like a three-legged stool: cost, security and trust. Cost is the biggest short-term risk to scaling AI, because its drivers compound with adoption. Trustworthiness and security are permanent risks. They are never finished.

The session drew value and risk as a bow tie. Work done before launch, in design, testing and controls, brings risk down and value up until a system is ready to scale. But if you only do the upfront work, you will fail. After launch, management has to run continuously, keeping value up and risk down. And because machines are now doing the work, policy is becoming code: rules written so software can check and enforce them, rather than in a document someone hopes everyone has read.

Integrity can be measured

The responsible AI session’s contribution was a hierarchy of intent, and a way to put a return on each level.

The lower two levels are driven from outside: they aim to avoid trouble. The upper two are driven from inside: they aim to achieve something. Each has its own cost of getting it wrong and its own measures:

Goal The cost of getting it wrong What to measure
Compliance About $18 billion in privacy fines worldwide over a decade Cost of compliance; exposure (fines × probability); cost of each regulatory inquiry
Risk About $6 million average cost of a data breach, before fines Share of cases classed as high risk; time to resolve unintended consequences; model drift; cost of AI insurance, which is rising
Differentiation 92% of green start-ups failed: being ethical is not enough on its own Share of revenue influenced by your ethical position; NPS; partner-of-choice ranking; time from insight to implementation, because good guardrails increase speed
Values Loss of trust, talent and brand Whether people feel safe raising concerns; ability to attract talent; customer and employee retention based on trust

The measure in that table I find most interesting runs against intuition: guardrails can increase speed. A team that knows exactly where the boundaries are moves faster inside them than one that has to ask permission each time. It is the keynote’s brakes on a car again.

Two more lines from the session. “Yes, but it is legal” is not a great response. And, for the sceptic who asks what happens if there is no business return at all:

Its practical advice was concrete. Start by writing down your must-avoid outcomes. Set up a responsible AI advisory board that reviews use cases. Monitor continuously for unintended consequences. One sponsor reduced operational trust to a single question for every AI system: does it have an owner and a risk class, and can we stop it?

Two of the symposium’s predictions suggest who will enforce all this. By 2030, 80% of the world’s largest companies will make their CIO or chief AI officer the evidence custodian for responsible AI: the person who can prove what an AI system did, and why. And by 2030, Gartner expects insurers rather than regulators to drive AI governance, because the underwriting standards for AI liability insurance will be stricter than most rules. Governance on paper will not get you cover. Governance in the running system will.

The rules are multiplying, and banks sit in the bullseye

More than 1,800 AI laws, regulations and formal guidelines are now in play, nearly double the count a year earlier. Across regimes the structure repeats: a definition of AI, tiers of risk, and obligations. The high-risk tier almost always includes creditworthiness and lending decisions, alongside insurance, employment and biometrics. The regulation session’s reframing applies to every regulated business: the goal is no longer regulatory compliance; it is regulatory resilience. You will not keep pace with every rule. You can build an organisation that adapts to new ones without starting over.

The trust recession, measured

The government session gave the clearest evidence I saw all week of what the keynote meant by a trust recession. Citizens who trust their government to handle their personal data responsibly and securely fell from 49% in 2025 to 37% in 2026. Those with little or no trust rose from 17% to 25%. Citizens’ willingness to use AI chatbots for government services fell year on year in every service area surveyed, and for some, such as the legal and justice system and public safety, more people are unwilling than willing. Advanced AI companies rank low among the institutions people trust to act in the public’s interest.

Meanwhile, governments are increasing AI funding to drive efficiency, because of staff shortages and service backlogs. The bet depends on citizens adopting something they increasingly do not trust. The session’s answer has three parts that apply well beyond government. Link AI performance to the mission, through a chain of measures that runs from technology metrics, through operational outcomes, to public value. Choose the right mix of people and AI: some techniques, such as rules and classical machine learning, are much easier to explain than large language models, so match the technique to how much explanation the decision needs, and define what people do at each tier of risk. And keep control visible: make decisions open, challengeable and proportionate to risk.

The decisionEntry 22 · Ch. 22

Write down your must-avoid outcomes, review every agent against them, name the custodian of the evidence, and measure trust as carefully as you measure adoption.

⁂

Integrity used to be judged by people. Increasingly, it will be judged by software acting on their behalf.

Part VII·Trust—Chapter 23

The Customer Arrives as an Agent

Chapter 23 of 27 · 2 min read

The question this chapter answers

What happens to your business when customers send their AI instead of coming themselves?

The session I expect to be quoted most was about the AI customer: a person who uses AI to act on their behalf. Today, 60% of business buyers consult AI when purchasing. After a purchase, customers are three times more likely to ask a third-party AI for help than the company’s own. Gartner predicts that by 2030, half of inbound customer service interactions will be started by personal AI agents.

The economics underneath are hard to ignore. Customer effort has always been a hidden subsidy. “I haven’t got time to shop around” means the list price gets paid. “I can’t face renegotiating” means the renewal goes unchallenged. When an agent does that effort for free, those subsidies disappear.

For banks this is not a customer experience topic. It is a question of mandates, authentication and fraud. When my agent negotiates with your agent, whose mandate governs, and what evidence proves that a person authorised the payment? The rails for that do not yet exist.

Banks are only starting to prepare. Asked how far they engage with or design for machine customers, a third of banking respondents reported early engagement and 27% growing engagement. Only 3% have scaled it, and a fifth do not expect to engage at all before the end of 2027. The public sector faces a blunter version of the problem. Gartner predicts that by the end of 2030, more than 10 billion autonomous agents created by people, companies and governments will clog public services, many of them built to pursue benefits and payouts at scale. Public services will need ways to check an agent’s authority before acting on its claims.

The decisionEntry 23 · Ch. 23

Design a front door for machine customers, with limited permissions and proof of mandate, before a large share of your service traffic starts with an agent.

⁂

When customers’ agents act on what they read, the truth itself becomes something to defend.

Part VII·Trust—Chapter 24

Truth as an Attack Surface

Chapter 24 of 27 · 3 min read

The question this chapter answers

How do you protect an enterprise when anything said about it can be faked at machine speed?

The disinformation session opened with Voltaire: “Those who can make you believe absurdities can make you commit atrocities.” Its claim was that disinformation is the biggest enterprise challenge hiding in plain sight. Digital platforms and AI have combined old propaganda techniques with deepfakes, mass personalisation, behavioural science and, now, global reach through agents.

It separated three kinds of information disorder. Misinformation is inaccurate, with no intent to harm. Disinformation is inaccurate and meant to harm. Malinformation is accurate, but used to harm.

The cases were sobering. Anonymously funded campaigns helped drive a plant-based food company’s sales and share price down. Unscientific claims helped turn opinion against major wind power projects before they were cancelled. Bot networks amplified a backlash against a brand’s new logo until the company reversed it at real cost, and its chief executive later resigned. The defenders who won filled the information space with a clear, factual story. One government answered rumours with shareable humour, and more than 22,000 volunteers across 13 countries organised factual counter-speech.

Executive committees are noticing. 44% say disinformation is an important issue they discuss, and 24% rank it among their top five concerns. Gartner expects enterprise spending on fighting it to pass $30 billion by 2028, taking about 10% of marketing and cybersecurity budgets. Yet only 37% have clear ways of dealing with it today.

Physical security and cybersecurity are no longer enough. The session argued for a third layer, disinformation security, and a discipline it called TrustOps: grounding the organisation’s own content in facts that can be verified, and debunking false content faster and more completely. Standards for proving where content came from, and open frameworks for describing influence operations and how to counter them, already exist. The first step is organisational: a trust council that brings together risk, IT, security, legal, communications, marketing and HR. The session argued the CIO is well placed to lead it.

For a bank, this is not a side issue for the communications team. A deepfaked executive or a fabricated rumour reaches customers, markets and other people’s agents at machine speed. In a world of AI customers and agents negotiating with agents, the ability to prove what is true becomes part of the product.

That is where trust stops being an ethical aspiration and becomes infrastructure. The organisation that can prove what its agents did, and why, will be the one other people’s agents are allowed to deal with.

The decisionEntry 24 · Ch. 24

Set up a trust council across risk, IT, security, legal, communications, marketing and HR, and build the capability to prove what is true about you.

⁂

That completes the seven scarce things. What remains is to look ahead, take stock, and decide.

Where is all of this heading over the next three to five years?

The symposium’s ten strategic predictions are a good place to start. Not one of them is about a better model. Every one lands on a scarce thing.

Gartner’s top strategic technology trends for 2027 point the same way. The session served them as a three-course menu, and every course is about making AI governable or changeable rather than simply more capable:

And the question the session closed on is the right one for any leadership team: which of these will you act on, which will you monitor, and which will you ignore?

The maverick view

Gartner also runs a deliberately contrarian line of research it calls Maverick: ideas with a low probability of happening and a high impact if they do, to be treated with caution. Its far-future session was the most entertaining hour of the week, and three of its ideas have practical consequences now.

What if, by 2030, a fifth of AI hallucinations turn out to be patterns we do not yet understand? The session’s comparison was earthquake lights, long dismissed as mass hallucination and later explained by electrical charges from tectonic stress. The suggested action: measure AI for novelty as well as accuracy, and study recurring anomalies rather than only filtering them out. What if, by 2030, four or more G7 countries tax tokens and the displacement of human work? Then make sure token consumption is properly accounted for and monitored now. And what if, through 2031, the EU mandates a schedule of controlled AI use, prioritising what AI may be used for? The advice was to move from optimising AI functionality to optimising AI optionality: architect for a multipolar world, keep a sovereignty strategy for cloud, data and AI, and keep open-weight models within reach.

Others were further out: a hyperscaler buying a whole country to house its data centres by 2032, robot sports becoming the most watched sport by 2035, and physical products that never reach end of life by 2033. I would not plan around them. I would keep them in the room, as a reminder that the next decade will not be a straight line from this one.

Epilogue—Chapter 26

What the Room Agreed, and What Nobody Said

Chapter 26 of 27 · 5 min read

The question this chapter answers

What was settled, what was missing, and what are the organisations doing this for real actually doing?

After more than 90 sessions, a couple of dozen analysts and a sponsor floor with plenty to sell, a short list of claims went uncontradicted.

The model is becoming a commodity, and advantage lives in the harness, the context and the controls around it. Context is the bottleneck, and it has to be engineered rather than improvised when the question arrives. Governance has to move from paper into the running system. Autonomy is a dial, set per action and earned with evidence. Volume is not value, and saved time is not saved cost. The real cost of AI is in running it and in changing how people work. A correction is coming, and options have to be paid for before it arrives. Attackers have AI too, but defenders can still win if they automate and rehearse recovery. The post-quantum migration should have started already. And the human role is moving up, not out.

The agreement was real. So were the silences.

What nobody said out loud

  1. Who owns the context layer, who pays for it, and how do you measure it? Everyone called context critical infrastructure. Nobody named its budget, its owner, or a measure of its quality, coverage and freshness. The 2027 trends said to hold business owners accountable for context quality, and the governance session’s trust models offer the start of a measure. The budget is still open, and infrastructure without a budget is a project.
  2. Memory was treated as a risk, not yet as a design. “Memory as moat” appeared as one bullet on one slide, and the banking trends session warned that an agent treats a data lake as its living memory. No session treated agent memory as something to design and govern: what an agent should remember, for how long, and who can make it forget. Without that, every agent starts each day as a new hire, and the organisation pays what I have called the Amnesia Tax.
  3. The exit from a model. Everyone said to keep options open. Nobody explained how to leave a model you have built prompts, evaluations and agents around. If a supplier is repriced, bought or withdrawn in the coming correction, what transfers? Portability has to cover prompts, evaluations and agent configurations, not just data.
  4. Evaluating systems, not agents. The telephone game was shown as a warning, and evaluation-driven development as a practice. Every tool on screen was built for one agent. There is still no accepted way to evaluate a system of agents, where errors compound and responsibility is shared.
  5. Trust between agents across company lines. When my agent negotiates with yours, whose mandate governs, what evidence proves a person authorised the action, and who is liable when it goes wrong? For banks this is a mandate, fraud and dispute problem today, not a 2030 problem.
  6. Liability for role agents. The symposium introduced agents that own outcomes, then asked who is responsible when one goes rogue, and left the question there. Until contracts and regulation catch up, every role agent needs a named human owner who accepts the outcome.
  7. Incident response for agents. The security sessions covered discovery, identity and runtime guardrails well. What was missing was the playbook for when it goes wrong: what counts as an agent incident, how you detect one, who is on call, and how you replay what the agent saw and decided. If you cannot replay it, you cannot defend it.
  8. Running the apprenticeship. If agents do the junior work, the senior judges of 2036 have to learn somewhere. The job chaos session said where to invest: graduate programmes for complex jobs learned by osmosis. Nobody yet showed how to run one when agents do the work juniors used to learn from.
  9. A playbook for board AI literacy. Only 31% of directors believe they can oversee AI. The cyber session taught technology leaders to be sense-makers for security. Nobody offered the same for AI itself, and the playbook would transfer almost word for word.
  10. The view from strength. Sovereignty was told almost entirely as a European defensive problem. If watts decide where you can build, the energy-rich regions building sovereign AI from the ground up deserve their own chapter.

What the organisations doing this for real are doing

The analysts described where the market should go. The organisations I see getting traction with agents in regulated industries share a pattern, and it is closer to the keynote than to the expo floor.

They build one control plane rather than many disconnected agents: one place where every agent is registered, given an identity, routed to a model, logged and billed. They start from decisions, not use cases: who decides, on what evidence, with what authority, at what cost of being wrong. They set autonomy per action, not per agent, so the same agent may draft a letter on its own, propose a limit change for approval and never touch a payment. They treat context capture as a product with an owner. They make evaluation the definition of done. They route models by job, and treat the router as the strategic asset. They put AI cost on the business’s own profit and loss, so whoever creates the spend sees it. And they aim at constraints rather than productivity: losses prevented, external spend brought in-house, buffers reduced.

None of it is glamorous. All of it is the difference between the one AI project in five that pays back and the other four.

Epilogue

Coda: What You Choose to Own

1 min read

For two years, the question that dominated every AI conversation was how capable the models are. Barcelona suggested that question is nearly settled, or at least no longer decisive. Capability arrives every month, from everyone, at a falling price.

The question that will decide the next phase is different: what do you own around the intelligence you rent? Your context, your decision logic, your controls, your error budgets, your security, your people’s judgement, your exits. Those are the scarce things. They do not arrive in a model release, and nobody can sell them to you.

Gaudí would have understood. He did not carve the shape of his arches. He designed the constraints and let the system find the form. And his vision outlived him only where someone had written down what he knew.

That is the work in front of every enterprise now: design the constraints, capture the knowledge, and let the intelligence do the rest.
