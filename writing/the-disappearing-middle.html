Context & knowledge

The Disappearing Middle

Models have started compressing the middle of their own context and retrieving inside it. Two things follow that almost nobody has priced in: the middle layer of your architecture is being absorbed, and your errors are moving somewhere your tests do not look.

Gagan Sachdeva · 12 September 2026 · 12 min read

In September a model appeared that holds a million tokens of conversation at 890 bytes per token. Three years ago the same lineage needed 389,120. Most commentary read this as a price story. It is not. It is a story about a machine that has quietly started doing, inside itself, the job that we built an entire industry layer to do outside it.

To see why that matters you need about ten minutes and no mathematics. I am going to build the idea in pictures, from the bottom, and then show you what it does to an enterprise.

How I am simplifying this, and what it costs you

I am reducing a genuinely complicated subject to one mechanism, one change, and four consequences. You should know what I am throwing away.

  • I am ignoring benchmark scores entirely. They are vendor-run and will be argued about for weeks. Nothing here depends on them.
  • I am treating several distinct engineering advances — sparse attention, four-bit storage, layers sharing state, splitting the model into a reading half and a writing half — as one sentence: the model's working memory got smaller and more selective.
  • I am naming no vendor as a recommendation. One company is furthest along a path everyone is walking. The path is the subject.
Part one · from first principles

What the machine is actually doing

Type The capital of France is and the model must produce Paris. To do that, it looks backward over everything you wrote and decides which of it matters. That operation is attention.

Which raises the obvious question. If each new word requires looking back at everything, does the model re-read the whole conversation every time? It did once. The fix is the single most consequential piece of plumbing in modern AI.

The lookup itself is worth seeing once, because everything later depends on it. The token being written poses a question. Every earlier token has already advertised what it is about, and what it holds.

Why the size of that notebook decides your economics

A graphics processor's fast memory holds two things: the model's weights, which are fixed, and the caches of every conversation currently running, which are not. The weights are what the model knows. The caches are what it is thinking about right now. Only the second one scales with your users.

There is a second effect, less discussed and arguably larger. To produce each token the processor must physically pull the cache across a memory bus. A smaller cache means fewer bytes moved per word generated. Since generation is bound by how fast memory can be read rather than by arithmetic, this lands directly on latency and throughput.

Part two · what actually changed

The model started forgetting selectively

The breakthrough is not compression. It is uneven compression. Recent context is kept in full detail. Older context is compressed. Very old context is compressed hard. The model is no longer treating all of its past as equally worth holding.

Uneven is the operative word, and the reason is worth stating bluntly. Consider the instruction transfer AED 10,000, not AED 100,000. Summarise that neighbourhood carelessly and you can lose the word not. So local detail is protected while distance is where the squeezing happens.

Alongside this sits a second change with the same spirit. Reading a prompt and writing an answer are different jobs, and were always charged at the same rate. The newest designs split the model, activating roughly half as much machinery to read as to write. Long inputs became cheap; hard outputs stayed expensive. That is precisely the shape of agent work, where the input is enormous and the output is a paragraph.

Part three · the thing nobody has priced in

The model has started doing its own searching

if you read one sentence in this piece

Your search system and the model's attention are now doing the same job, and only one of them is on your payroll.

To see why that is true rather than clever, you need to know what attention used to do, because that is the part I left out and it is the part that makes the claim land.

Originally, attention did something no human would ever do. For every single word it wrote, it looked at every word that came before, and gave each one a weight. Not a sample. Not the important ones. All of them, every time. It was exhaustive by design, and that exhaustiveness was the whole idea — the model never had to decide what to look at, because it looked at everything.

That is what has stopped. Modern attention selects first. It keeps a compressed index of what it has already read, scores the question against that index, pulls back the fragments that matter, and attends mainly to those. It no longer considers everything. It chooses.

Choosing a few relevant things out of very many is not a new operation. It is the definition of search. And it happens to be the operation you have spent the last two years building a platform to perform.

The same question, asked three times in three years

Make it concrete. You want one answer out of a six-hundred-page credit file.

2023

The file does not fit. Somebody chooses twenty pages, by hand or by rule. Whatever they leave out, the model never sees, and nobody records what was left out.

2024–2025 · the retrieval era

You build a platform. It cuts the file into passages, converts each into a vector, scores the question against all of them, reranks the best candidates and pastes ten passages into the prompt. The model sees ten passages and trusts your ranking completely. If your ranking was wrong, the model has no way to know, and neither do you.

2026

The whole file fits, for less than the ranking infrastructure used to cost. The model reads all six hundred pages, keeps a compressed index of what it has moved past, and when the question arrives it scores that index and pulls back what matters. Same four operations. Your ranking platform was not involved in any of them.

Retrieval was a workaround for a window that was too small and too expensive. The window is no longer either.

Now the correction, because "retrieval dies" would be wrong

A model can only search what it has been handed. If your corpus is forty million documents, something still has to decide which ones go in front of it, and that job is not going anywhere.

But look closely at what kind of job it has become. It is coarse. It is the right folder, not the best three paragraphs. And the expensive, sophisticated parts of a retrieval platform — chunking strategy, embedding model quality, hybrid search tuning, rerankers, top-k thresholds — all exist to deliver precision. Precision is exactly what the model has started supplying for itself.

So retrieval is not deleted. It is demoted, from a precision instrument to a coarse gate. That is a smaller claim than the one I could have made, and it is the one I can defend.

Notice what is in the right-hand column. Not one of those items is a search problem. They are entitlement, version control, provenance and audit — governance functions that we happened to implement inside retrieval systems, because retrieval was where the pipeline already ran. The model absorbing the search does not absorb any of them, and cheaper context does not touch them.

The distinction worth taking into your next architecture review

Finding the relevant thing is becoming a model capability. Assume it gets better, cheaper and eventually free, and plan for your investment in it to depreciate.

Deciding what may be shown, to whom, from where, and proving it afterwards is not becoming a model capability, and no amount of cache compression touches it. That is where the durable engineering sits.

Part four · what it changes for an enterprise

Is this a large change or a small one?

Both, depending on who you are, and it is worth saying that plainly rather than declaring that everything has changed.

If you build and sell inference, this is a large change and it lands immediately. If you are a bank, an insurer, a hospital or a retailer using these systems, the next twelve months hold five consequences. Two of them are large. Three are moderate. Only one has real money attached, which is why only one of them belongs in front of an executive committee this quarter.

Large · real money

1. The retrieval layer you built starts depreciating

You spent two years and a serious budget building machinery that finds the right paragraph and hands it to the model. The model is learning to do that itself.

You hired a research assistant to pre-select the three pages your expert should read, because the expert charged by the page and could only hold a few pages at a time. The expert can now read the whole file for almost nothing and find the right page unaided. The assistant's job shrinks. The librarian's job — who may see which file, which version is current, and proving afterwards what was consulted — does not shrink at all.

This is the large one because it is a multi-year platform investment whose central function is being commoditised by the thing it was built to serve.

who needs to think, and about what
Enterprise Architecture
Redraw the reference architecture with one line through it, separating finding from governing, and place every existing component on one side or the other. Anything on the finding side is now a depreciating asset.
Chief Data Office
Stop starting new work on chunking strategies, embedding models and rerankers beyond current commitments. Move that engineering capacity to entitlements, versioning, lineage and supersession, which no model will absorb.
Technology Procurement
Shorten contract terms on retrieval and vector infrastructure. A three-year lock-in on the finding side is now a bet against the direction of the technology.
CIO / COO office
Before the next planning round, ask for the AI platform budget split into those two categories. If nobody can produce that split, that is itself the finding.
Large · for long documents and agents

2. Wrong answers move to where you never test

The model keeps recent text word for word and older text as a compressed impression. The same sentence is therefore remembered differently depending on where it sat in the pile.

You read a four-hundred-page contract this morning. Ask about the final page and you quote it back exactly. Ask about page twelve and you give a confident paraphrase that is mostly right. Mostly right, delivered confidently, is the failure mode that survives review.

who needs to think, and about what
Model Risk Management
Add context depth to the validation protocol. Plant the same evidence shallow, middle and deep, record where accuracy begins to slip, and publish a supported depth as a stated model limitation. Today's validation packs do not contain this number.
Credit, Financial Crime, Onboarding
Find out what that supported depth is for the systems you already run, and stop operating above it. If a case file routinely exceeds it, the answer is to split the file, not to trust the summary.
Internal Audit
Add one field to the evidence you request on automated decisions: how deep in the context the supporting material sat. Without it you cannot distinguish a quoted clause from a reconstructed one.
Compliance
Treat supported context depth the way you treat any other disclosed model limitation, including in what you tell a regulator about how a decision was reached.
Moderate · slow, and awkward

3. Your compute excuse disappears

The same hardware now carries several times as many live conversations, so "we are waiting for capacity" stops being a true sentence.

You widened the road, and discovered the queue was never about the road. It was about how few destinations anyone had actually agreed on.

In fairness to the technology, this change only reveals the problem rather than causing it. Most organisations describe themselves as compute-constrained and are in fact specification-constrained.

who needs to think, and about what
Technology investment committee
Count the use cases specified well enough to automate today and set that number beside the hardware roadmap. A large gap means the roadmap was solving the wrong constraint.
Business line heads
The bottleneck has moved onto your side of the table. What is now scarce is a process described to the point of automation, with acceptance criteria and named exception handling. That is your work, not the technology team's.
CIO
Retire capacity as a stated reason for slow delivery, and be ready for the uncomfortable conversation that follows.
Moderate · infrastructure only

4. The stateless habit becomes the expensive one

Continuing a conversation on the machine that already holds its memory costs a small fraction of starting it again somewhere else. Where a request lands is now a cost decision.

Seeing the doctor who already has your file, rather than a new one who repeats every test. We deliberately built our systems to send you to a new doctor every time, because it made adding doctors easy.

In fairness, the serving stacks already support this, so it is a configuration and design choice rather than a research problem. What is new is that it belongs in an architecture standard instead of an operations runbook.

who needs to think, and about what
Platform Engineering
Turn on cache-aware routing and session affinity at the inference gateway, and make warm-cache lifetime an explicit, owned setting rather than a default nobody chose.
Enterprise Architecture
Your standard almost certainly says services must be stateless. It now needs a written exception for inference, with the reasoning attached, or teams will keep optimising against the wrong principle.
FinOps / Technology Finance
Put cache hit rate on the cost dashboard next to spend. It is the single number that explains most of the variance in what an agent costs to run.
Moderate · easiest to fix

5. Your cost metric stops telling you anything

Cost per million tokens will now fall on its own, whatever you do. An improvement in that number has stopped being evidence of good management.

Fuel got cheaper. That does not mean your drivers found better routes.

who needs to think, and about what
Technology Finance / FP&A
Replace cost per million tokens with cost per completed task, and ask for the share of context served warm. Both numbers exist today and neither is usually reported.
AI / Transformation leadership
Separate the floor from your contribution before claiming either. Some of the improvement on your slide is the whole industry's price falling underneath you.
Board and ExCo reporting
Show the two side by side for a few quarters: what the market gave you, and what your own work added. Only the second is a performance number.

If you take one thing from this section: the first two are where the money and the risk sit. The other three are real, but they are hygiene. A platform budget being quietly overtaken by the models it buys from, and a class of error that hides exactly where nobody tests, are the two that justify a meeting.

Part five · consistency

What I have argued before, and why this confirms it

I have approached this problem from several directions over the past year. This release does not overturn any of those arguments. It removes the variable that made them hard to test, which is a different and better thing. Each piece below carries its claim in one line, so you can take the summary or open it.

Capability → value

The Capability–Value Gap

The claim: model capability rises far faster than the value organisations realise from it, and the gap between the two is not a model problem.

A direct test. Capability and cost both improved sharply this month. If realised value moves in step over the next two quarters, the argument was wrong. Absorption of the retrieval layer will make the gap harder to see before it makes it smaller.

Context & knowledge

The Empty Window

The claim: a bigger context window does not produce a better system, because the knowledge that decides outcomes sits with experienced operators rather than in documents.

The strongest available test, and the argument stands. The window is now nearly free to fill and the model will even organise its contents for you. Neither of those supplies judgement about what belongs in it.

Context & knowledge

The Search Tax

The claim: how much a system searches measures the deficit in the knowledge layer beneath it. Search is the tax you pay for not knowing where the answer lives.

Sharpened rather than revised, and this is the important one. The search does not stop. It moves inside the model, where you cannot see it, meter it, or audit it. An invisible tax is still a tax, and it is harder to argue for fixing.

Memory

The Amnesia Tax

The claim: organisations pay a recurring cost for what their systems and their people forget between one encounter and the next.

Half of it gets paid down. Mechanical memory, holding a session across days, is now cheap. Institutional memory — what the organisation learned and why it chose as it did — is not the kind of thing a cache holds, and is untouched.

None of those positions needs revising, which is worth saying plainly. The temptation with a release like this is to announce that everything has changed. One constraint moved. The arguments that were never about that constraint have simply become testable.

Where I could be wrong

The compression claims come from the companies that built the compression, and no independent group has yet published depth-dependent accuracy results for these architectures. If it turns out that heavily compressed distant context degrades badly on precise retrieval, the absorption argument weakens considerably, because external retrieval will still be the only reliable way to put an exact clause in front of a model. The vendor concerned says as much itself, flagging robustness boundaries it has not fully characterised. That is the number I would want before committing a roadmap, and it is the number nobody is publishing.

What is not in doubt is the direction. For three years the price of holding context has fallen by roughly an order of magnitude every eighteen months, and the techniques are published openly. The model is learning to decide what deserves its attention. The open question is whether the organisations using it will learn the same thing, or simply pour everything in now that it is cheap.
